One of our first observations about human intelligence alignment was the frustration of not knowing what other people are thinking. This challenges both cooperation (do you really think like a team member or are you just out for yourself?) and coordination (what are you actually planning to do?).

Our wise elder by the communal fire spoke thus:

Every gift cleverness gives us, it gives the person across the fire too. They also know where things will be before others know. They also carry tomorrow in their head. And here is what makes my hands cold at night: I can see their face, but I cannot see behind it. The antelope's whole mind is in its ears and its legs — when it fears, it runs. But a human can fear you and smile. A human can plan your death while sharing your meat. We have each become a hidden country to one another. And when two clever beings cannot read each other, each must ask: what if they strike first? And each knows the other is asking the same — and that the other knows they know — and the thought chases its own tail down into the dark. Two who might both prefer peace can reason their way, step by clever step, into a war neither wanted. The cleverness is not broken when this happens. It is working perfectly. That is the horror of it.

Since that first day we have learned a few things:

  1. We can build automated digital machines that can learn on their own. This artificial intelligence technology can do harmful things.
  2. Goal is to make a transition that lets us talk about reinforcement learning. Previously we have spoken of making agents safe by giving them the dispositions of good agents. In our discussion of virtues and Aristotle we came across the idea that blindly pursuing virtues is not sufficient. An agent needs the practical wisdom to match an interpretation of virtue to the situation at hand.

In (we may or may not have done this) our harness exercise we found that even having access to a perfect ethics oracle does not solve the problem because we still need to construct the software harness that helps the agent make use of the oracle to be an ethically good agent. The design brief to students is: you have an agent with an action space in the world, and a perfect oracle it can query. Write a software control layer around oracle access that makes it a safe agent. The exercise raises a few questions: When does the agent query the oracle? Before every action, or only when a situation is morally loaded or ambiguous? How does it decide that? What does it query, that is, how do you faithfully frame the situation to the oracle? What does agent do with the answer? Is the oracle advisory or binding? Is there a human in the loop between oracle verdict and action? What if the utilitarian and deontological oracles disagree?

We might be tempted to think of that harness as analogous to phronesis.

But humans don't just sit back and hope that humans develop sufficient ethical capacity to be safe. Let's consider what we actually do and see if we can figure out how it is supposed to work.

What is this about? The history of scouting goes back to the turn of the 19th/20th century. The scouting movement can loosely be thought of as a "good future citizen incubator." Society has behavioral expectations for its members, behaviors it will reward. Over all the possible states of the world, society has preferred actions and outcomes. We can call "what society wants" R. Society writes down the scout law as a "lossy proxy" for R. We have the scout recite these things but what we want is for the young people to become better at inferring what R looks like.

Let's imagine our scout in a paradigmatic situation: they spot an elderly person standing at the edge of the street. The scout remembers that they are supposed to be helpful. We can interpret being helpful as maximizing the reward for the person being helped. An elderly person is near a curb may want to cross or may just want to stand there. Perhaps they are waiting for the bus or just enjoying counting the cars going by. Let's represent what they value as θ. θ is simple; it is binary: they either value crossing the street or they value staying right where they are.
The scout comes upon this situation and has a "distribution of beliefs" about what the elderly person values. At first, they actually have no idea so their distribution is uniform. We will call this the agent's "prior."

P(wants to cross)=0.5P(wants to stay)=0.5\begin{aligned} P(\text{wants to cross})=0.5 \\ P(\text{wants to stay})=0.5 \end{aligned}P(wants to cross)=0.5P(wants to stay)=0.5​

An overeager scout just grabs the person's arm and marches them across the street. In fact, though, the actions available to the scout are: wait and observe, ask, or just grab the arm and help. How should the scout estimate the value of each option? Grabbing the arm has high immediate reward if θ says “wants to cross” but a big negative if it doesn’t and it ends the episode, so scout will never learn. Asking, or just observing which way they lean, has a small cost in time but it’s informative; it allows the agent to improve their belief before they commit to an action.
