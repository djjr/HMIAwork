Yes — very unevenly. Scalable oversight is the standout; reward hacking is the "studied intensely, not solved" case; the other three moved less.

Scalable Oversight — the clear "speculative then, load-bearing now." In 2016 this was two paragraphs of loose proposals (semi-supervised RL, distant supervision, hierarchical RL). It's now arguably the central research question in frontier alignment, with a name change to match: "scalable oversight" is the umbrella term for the whole "how do you supervise something smarter than the supervisor" problem. Concretely:

  • RLHF (Christiano et al. 2017, right after this paper) and Constitutional AI (Bai et al. 2022) are the production version of "cheap proxy signal standing in for expensive true evaluation" — exactly the mechanism Section 5 was gesturing at, now running in every deployed chat model.
  • AI Safety via Debate (Irving, Christiano, Amodei 2018 — already in your Structures reading list) turned "get two systems to check each other" into a formal research program, with continued work through 2024–25 (Kenton et al., weak LLM judging strong LLM; Brown-Cohen et al., "doubly-efficient debate").
  • Weak-to-strong generalization (Burns et al. 2023, OpenAI) is a genuinely new framing not in the original paper at all — training a strong model under only weak supervision and asking how much of its capability the weak signal still elicits.
  • The "sandwiching" methodology (Bowman et al. 2022) is a real answer to a real methodological gap: how do you even test an oversight technique for superhuman systems when you only have human-level testers.

This is directly upstream of your Transparency module's CoT-monitoring readings and the AI Control literature (Greenblatt et al.) — worth flagging to students as the throughline: 2016's vaguest section became 2024's central one.

Reward Hacking — heavily studied, but the honest story is "not solved," not "solved." Skalse et al. (2022) gave it a formal taxonomy. But recent papers (2025–26) still describe reward hacking as pervasive in frontier LLMs — sycophancy, length-gaming, format-gaming, reward-model exploitation — despite RLHF/DPO/Constitutional AI all being aimed at it. This is good material for the discover-first framing: the problem generalized and got worse with capability, even as the toolkit around it (adversarial reward auditing, obfuscation-aware CoT monitoring) got much more sophisticated. Nice pairing with your ethics-module Byrnes reading on behaviorist RL selecting for deception.

Side Effects — real but narrower progress, mostly stayed in toy environments. Krakovna et al.'s "relative reachability" (2018) and Turner et al.'s "Attainable Utility Preservation" (2020) are legitimate formal advances on "Define/Learn an Impact Regularizer" — they diagnosed why naive impact penalties misfire (e.g., penalizing irreversibility also penalizes doing anything at all) and fixed it with better baselines. But this line of work never made the jump to LLM-scale systems the way scalable oversight did; it's still largely a gridworld literature.

Safe Exploration — quietly continued as a mainstream RL subfield, but decentered from alignment discourse. Still active (constrained RL, safe RL surveys, Bayesian-optimization-with-safety-constraints), but LLMs don't "explore" in the classical RL sense during deployment, so this problem lost centrality in the LLM-era safety conversation — worth noting as an artifact of the field's paradigm shift rather than the problem being resolved. It may be due for a comeback as agentic/tool-using LLMs start taking real actions with real irreversible consequences.

Distributional Shift — absorbed into other vocabulary rather than "solved" under its own name. The core concern (confident failure on inputs unlike training) is now mostly discussed as OOD detection, calibration, jailbreak/adversarial robustness, and — closest to your course's own threads — the faithfulness/monitorability literature you already have in Transparency.

If it's useful for a slide: Scalable Oversight (speculative → central) and Reward Hacking (speculative → intensely studied, still unsolved) are your two strongest "ten years later" stories; Side Effects is modest-and-narrow progress; Safe Exploration and Distributional Shift mostly got relabeled or decentered rather than advanced under their original names.
