How Aligned RU Survey › Claude Summary

Five themes stand out, in order of frequency:

1. Audience-dependence gates compliance. By far the most common observation: checked items happened in front of people (strangers especially); unchecked items were flagged as "didn't come up" or "no one was watching." A cluster went further and inverted it by relationship-distance — norms are tighter with strangers and loosen with intimates (the student who shared controversial opinions with a roommate; the one who's sarcastic with close friends but not others). This is a live discovery of Coleman's closure argument and Ellickson's core claim — norm enforcement requires information to circulate back to someone who cares, so norm-strength tracks monitorability, not conviction.

2. Automaticity, not deliberation. Repeated language: "I didn't decide," "invisible while I'm doing it," "reflex," "habit." Several explicitly contrast this with a picture of alignment as values-driven choice — the behaviors are pre-conscious, not the output of moral reasoning in the moment. This is Henrich's cultural-learning claim in miniature: the norm-following came from years of reinforcement, not real-time deliberation.

3. Performance/mask vs. genuine state. A large minority frame checked items as suppression rather than action — holding back a sarcastic remark, feigning interest, saying "it's fine," hiding disagreement. Several are uneasy about what this means: is polite behavior "real" alignment or just successful concealment of a different internal state? A few use almost exactly your control-vs-transparency vocabulary unprompted (one literally says you can get reliable behavior from an agent whose inner state you can't inspect — reassuring, but also a warning, since the behavior degrades when unmonitored).

4. A cost gradient. Cheap, low-stakes compliance (saying good morning, waiting turns, common courtesies) is near-universal. Costlier or unmonitored prosociality — following a rule with no observer, cleaning a shared space, telling an uncomfortable truth instead of a kind lie — drops off sharply. Several name this explicitly as the dividing line between what they checked and didn't.

5. A dissenting minority. A handful reject the cynical reading — framing their checked items as genuine empathy/self-restraint rather than reputation management, or noting conformity is "purposeful" but diminishes without external monitoring (one literally frames it as pseudo-self vs. real-self tug-of-war). Worth not smoothing these over; they're the ones pushing back against the majority's audience-dependence story rather than restating it.

Net effect, if you're using this for the groups/norms segment: your students independently reconstructed the mechanism your reading list documents (Ellickson/Coleman/Fehr-Gächter — enforcement contingent on visibility and reputational cost, not internalized conviction) before being handed any of that vocabulary. That's a clean discover-first hook — you could open the norms segment by feeding two or three of these back to them and asking them to name what mechanism they just described, rather than introducing Ellickson cold.
