How Aligned RU Survey › ChatGPT Summary
  1. Social norms and shared expectations are the main alignment mechanism.
    This is overwhelmingly the biggest theme. Students repeatedly describe an “unspoken social code,” “societal expectations,” “what society wants,” manners, politeness, rules, and accepted behavior. Alignment means knowing what one is supposed to do and generally doing it.
  2. Much alignment is internalized and automatic rather than consciously chosen.
    A striking number notice that they did these things almost reflexively: waiting their turn, saying hello, making eye contact, paying, restraining comments. They don't experience themselves as calculating whether to comply. The norm has become a habit. One response captures the idea especially well: the behaviors were “invisible to me while I'm doing them.”
  3. Reputation and impression management matter enormously.
    Another very strong cluster says, in effect, I behaved this way because of how other people would see me. Students mention appearing nice, normal, intelligent, friendly, trustworthy, not weird, not gross, etc. So alignment is partly produced by anticipated social evaluation rather than simply by moral conviction.
  4. Alignment often consists of restraint and self-suppression.
    Many responses focus less on performing good acts than on not doing disruptive ones: holding back sarcasm, controversial opinions, irritation, rude comments, bodily impulses, or “true feelings.” Alignment here looks like inhibition: the socially aligned human continually filters what they might otherwise say or do.
  5. Consideration, empathy, kindness, and conflict avoidance are a distinct mechanism.
    Not everyone interprets the behavior cynically as conformity. Many say they were thinking about other people's comfort or feelings, avoiding offense, reciprocating kindness, or maintaining relationships. So there is a real tension in the responses between “I conform because I want to look good” and “I conform because I care what happens to others.”
  6. Alignment is contextual rather than uniform.
    Several notice that norms change with audience and relationship: strangers versus close friends, public versus private, roommates versus larger groups. Sarcasm or controversial opinions may be perfectly acceptable with intimates but inappropriate elsewhere. That is a fairly sophisticated observation: human alignment is not simply obedience to one universal rule set; it involves figuring out which local norm applies here.

There are two smaller but especially interesting themes I would preserve because they complicate the story.

Behavioral alignment does not imply motivational alignment. Several students explicitly notice that the same outwardly aligned behavior could come from genuine concern, fear of judgment, habit, self-interest, or deliberate fakery. That is probably the richest AI-alignment connection in the responses: observable compliant behavior does not tell you what is going on “inside.”

And alignment gets weaker when it is costly or unobserved. A few responses distinguish the cheap, automatic behaviors from things requiring real effort or compliance when nobody is watching. That's almost a mini-theory of alignment robustness: easy norms are highly reliable; costly norms reveal the limits.

So if I were compressing the whole exercise into one slide, I'd probably make the central finding:

Students see human alignment as surprisingly strong—but produced by a mixture of internalized norms, reputation, empathy, and self-restraint, and highly dependent on context and cost.

And then underneath it I might put a provocative implication:

Humans appear more behaviorally aligned than motivationally aligned.

That distinction seems to be latent in an unusually large fraction of these responses.
