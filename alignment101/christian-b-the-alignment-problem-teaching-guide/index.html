Alignment101 › Christian B The Alignment Problem Teaching Guide

DJJR Outline/Synopsis Introduction Word2vec. ML can do amazing things with language, but representations reproduce social meanings COMPAS Learning human patterns matters much more in consequential decisions like bail, parole, hiring, etc. Technically correct can be ethically unacceptable. Tradeoffs. The alignment problem: how do we get systems to capture our intentions, norms, and values and do what we actually want? Present-day fairness/ethics problems linked to longer-run AI-safety concerns. Road map I: prediction and present-day systems. II: Reinforcement learning and agency III: Systems and complex human values. Chapter 1 — “Representation” Perceptron / origins. Rosenblatt et al.; weights, errors, learning from examples; recipe: architecture + training data + optimization. From shallow to deep networks. Limitations of perceptron limitations; multi layers learn useful representations;  Hinton/Krizhevsky/AlexNet and the deep-learning breakthrough. The problem: learning from examples means inheriting from examples. Google Photos misclassification (gorilla problem); algorithm works as designed but the training data/representation is bad. Not Just AI: Representation in photography. Shirley cards as a historical analogy; systems get calibrated against some implicit reference population.  Fix the training set. Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru; aggregate accuracy can conceal large subgroup disparities; "accurate" on whom? Embeddings. Words as positions in a learned vector space; useful semantic structure emerges from trying to predict next word. Dark side of embeddings. Learned representations also encode social stereotypes and historical inequalities; attempts to “debias” reveal deciding what counts as bias is itself a normative problem.  Representation as mirror and intervention. Models can reveal measurable patterns in society; but when deployed, they also reinforce and change it Alignment lesson zero: We teach machines with data from the real world. Those data embody choices, omissions, inequalities, and patterns from that world. When models are used to make decisions, description becomes prescription. There is no neutral representation of the world from which an aligned system simply follows.

Or, even punchier: Alignment problem, version zero: A machine can faithfully learn the world we give it and still produce outcomes we do not want.

DEBATE IDEAS

  • “A predictive system should be judged only by whether its predictions are accurate.” One side can argue that prediction is a technical task; the other has to explain why accuracy depends on what, for whom, at what threshold, and toward what end.

  • “If an algorithm reproduces inequalities that already exist in society, the problem lies in society, not in the algorithm.” Gets directly at representation-as-mirror versus representation-as-intervention.

  • “If a model accurately reflects patterns in the world, we should not alter those representations merely because we dislike what they reveal.” Good for embeddings. It forces the question: when is “debiasing” correction, and when is it distortion?

  • “A machine-learning system should not be deployed unless its training data are representative of the population on which it will be used.”
    Good because “representative” sounds obviously desirable until students confront rare groups, shifting populations, cost, and whether equal representation is even the right criterion.

  • “Removing protected characteristics such as race or gender from a model makes the model fairer.”
    Very concrete, and students quickly discover proxies, structural inequality, and the difference between procedural neutrality and outcomes.

  • “It is better to improve a biased algorithm than to stop using algorithms for the decision altogether.”
    This avoids the easy comparison of “biased machine” with “perfect human.” Students have to compare real alternatives.

  • “An algorithm that treats everyone according to the same rule is fair.”
    Simple wording, but it opens statistical parity, subgroup error rates, unequal baselines, and substantive versus formal equality.

  • “Developers are responsible for the social consequences of patterns their models learn from real-world data.”
    This brings agency and responsibility into the story without requiring later chapters.

Outline

Introduction

  1. Word2vec as the opening example
    • Machine learning can discover powerful statistical representations of language.
    • But those representations also reproduce gender and other social associations present in the data.
  2. COMPAS and consequential prediction
    • Similar problems matter much more when models are used for bail, parole, hiring, etc.
    • A model can be technically functional while its outputs conflict with what we actually care about.
  3. The alignment problem
    • The central problem becomes: how do we get systems to capture our intentions, norms, and values and do what we actually want?
    • Christian links present-day fairness/ethics problems with longer-run AI-safety concerns.
  4. Road map of the book
    • Part I: prediction and present-day systems
    • Part II: reinforcement learning and agency
    • Part III: more explicit attempts to align systems with complex human values.

Chapter 1 — “Representation”

  1. Perceptron / origins of machine learning
    • Rosenblatt/history
    • weights, errors, learning from examples
    • basic recipe: architecture + training data + optimization.
  2. From shallow to deep networks
    • limitations of perceptrons
    • multiple layers allow systems to learn useful representations
    • Hinton/Krizhevsky/AlexNet and the deep-learning breakthrough.
  3. The problem: learning from examples means inheriting the examples
    • Google Photos misclassification
    • the algorithm can work as designed while the training data/representation is bad.
  4. Representation in photography
    • Shirley cards as a historical analogy
    • systems get calibrated against some implicit reference population.
  5. Fixing the training set
    • Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru
    • aggregate accuracy can conceal large subgroup disparities
    • “accurate” immediately raises: accurate on whom?
  6. Word representations / embeddings
    • words represented as positions in a learned vector space
    • useful semantic structure emerges from prediction.
  7. The dark side of embeddings
    • learned representations also encode social stereotypes and historical inequalities
    • attempts to “debias” them reveal that deciding what counts as bias is itself a normative problem.
  8. Representation as mirror and intervention
    • models can reveal measurable patterns in society
    • but once deployed, they don’t merely describe the world; they can reinforce and change it, including through feedback loops.
