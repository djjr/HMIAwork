Discussion Analysis: "Mirror or Intervention?"

Topic: "If an algorithm or model reproduces inequalities or biases that already exist in society, the problem lies in society, not in the algorithm."

Source: Canvas discussion 1334659, 22 entries (1 deleted by author, leaving 21 usable). No entries in this topic were flagged as pasted/AI-generated text.

Student identifiers below are the anonymized Canvas entry IDs (e.g., 8n9jy), not names. Note: pseudonyms are stable within a topic but do not carry across the three discussions, so a given code here is not the same student as the same-looking code in another topic's file.

YES: De-biasing is a fool's errand — the problem is society's, not the algorithm's

Five recurring moves make up nearly all of the YES case.

1. The model is a mirror; "fixing" it just hides the underlying problem. This is the dominant argument, appearing in some form in over half of all responses. A model "accurately capturing society's existing state" (8nla6) means forcing it to look unbiased "conceals societal failures rather than fixing them" (8nby4) — it doesn't repair anything, it just makes the evidence disappear (8n9jy, 8nnie, 8nqyx, 8nu9j, 8nwzx, 8nby4, 8np3p, 8nty5, 8odwt, 8oeok, 8njiy, 8nboy). Several frame this as actively useful: "models help us visualize this bias, and we shouldn't shun away from it" (8o02p).

2. There is no neutral, universal definition of "fair" to debias toward. A smaller but sharper cluster argues that any attempt to remove bias necessarily substitutes someone's subjective judgment for the data. "Different definitions of fairness are fundamentally incompatible," so debiasing "forces developers to secretly impose their own unaccountable political choices" (8nby4); trying to force a model to be "fair" is "impos[ing] a subjective definition of fairness" (8np3p, 8nwzx).

3. Bias in training data is unavoidable, so a bias-free model may be neither achievable nor accurate. Because models learn from human-generated data, "a degree of bias is inevitable" (8o0c1), and scrubbing it out risks producing a model that reflects "a utopian society without bias, while the reality is not" (8nty5) — a "neutral" model can itself misrepresent reality, e.g. if a real profession is genuinely 90% one gender (8odwt cited as a NO rebuttal to this same case, showing the two sides arguing over the same example). One response frames bias as a feature, not a bug: AI should share humanity's "implicit biases" if it wants to be "useful to us" (8nn8s).

4. Responsibility lies upstream, with society, not the tool. The prescription that follows from the above: "the change starts with humans, and the environment they create" (8nwzx); we should "focus on treating the root cause of the issue and not the symptoms" (8nu9j).

5. Bias in models can self-correct as society changes. One detailed response points to word-association shifts (e.g., words linked to different ethnic groups between 1910 and 1980) as evidence that a model mirroring society will naturally reflect improvement as society improves, without needing direct intervention (8n91i).

NO: Machines don't just reflect bias, they amplify and entrench it — preventing that should be a priority

The NO side is built almost entirely around one central claim, developed through several distinct mechanisms.

1. Scale and amplification: mirrors don't act on the world, but deployed models do. This is the argument nearly every NO response makes in some form: an algorithm "can amplify existing biases and spread them at a much larger scale" (8n9jy), turning a passive reflection into an active force — "reproducing or amplifying it at scale creates new harm distinct from the original inequality" (8nla6, echoed by 8nnie, 8nqyx, 8nboy, 8n91i, 8nwzx, 8nby4, 8np3p, 8nty5, 8odwt, 8oeok, 8oh44, 8njiy).

2. Feedback loops that lock in today's bias for the long term. Several responses go further than "amplify" to describe a compounding mechanism: biased outputs become tomorrow's training data, so models "actively manufacture reality" rather than just reporting it (8nby4). One response adds a durability angle specific to real deployments: "ML models being trained today might still be in production in 50 years," so failing to debias now risks "locking in today's biases for decades" (8odwt).

3. Automated bias is harder to notice or resist than a biased individual. A related but distinct concern: as reliance on models grows, "people trust the model or algorithm blindly" (8nqju), and once bias is embedded at the infrastructure level it becomes both faster-moving and less visible than the ordinary, slower drift of human social change (8njiy: machines can make the problem worse "much faster... than natural changes led by humans in history").

4. Developers have the tools to intervene, so not intervening is itself a choice with consequences. Multiple responses reject the "it's not the algorithm's fault" framing on the grounds that fixing bias is within developers' technical reach and therefore within their responsibility: "developers have the tools to intervene at the model level, so declining to do so is itself a choice with consequences" (8nla6); "preventing the bias in the model is possible so the bias should be prevented" (8nnie); models can be "programmed" and biases "filter[ed] out" (8nx70).

5. Amplification causes real, distinct harm, so it can't be waved off as "just society's problem." Several responses point to concrete stakes to argue this isn't an abstract philosophical dispute: recidivism risk-assessment software used at scale in the justice system "impact[s] the fairness of the system and create[s] victims" (8n91i); Amazon's hiring model is cited as a case where bias was structurally amplified, not merely reflected (8nwzx, 8odwt).

---

What's notably absent or thin

  • Almost no response directly engages the deepest version of the YES case — that there may be no fact of the matter about what "unbiased" would even mean for many real predictive tasks (not just no political consensus, but no coherent target at all). Most NO responses answer "there's no neutral standard" with "amplification is bad regardless," which sidesteps rather than rebuts it.
  • The self-correction argument (8n91i, historical word-association shift) is unique in the set and never taken up or challenged by any other response — it's an empirical claim (models improve automatically as society improves) that could be contested on timescale grounds (decades vs. deployment lifespan) but no one does.
  • Very few responses distinguish between whether debiasing is technically possible and whether it's normatively desirable — these get treated as the same question by both sides, when they're logically separable (a model could be debiasable but the debiasing itself illegitimate, or vice versa).
