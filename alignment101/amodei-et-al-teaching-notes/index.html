Alignment101 › Amodei et al. Teaching Notes

We teach this paper in two sessions. The first focuses on the problems (reward hacking, scalable oversight, safe exploration, avoiding side effects, and robustness to domain shift). We make sure we understand what these refer to in the case of machine intelligence and we analogize each of them to humans, organizations, and experts.

In the second session we focus on the solutions and alignment mechanisms and research areas suggested by the authors. We analogize and make connections with five categories of generic alignment mechanisms: qualification, transparency, safety, structures, and control.

Learning outcome or rather class outcome is students being able to select one of the subtypes of one of these five as their lightning talk topic.

Impact Regularizers - Human Analogs

Amodei et al. (2016) describe impact regularizers as ways of discouraging AI systems from pursuing their objectives by causing large, unintended changes in the environment—e.g., a cleaning robot knocking over a vase just to move a box faster. The idea is to penalize “excessive impact” so that the system achieves goals with minimal side effects.

A human intelligence analogy is the way societies constrain people’s pursuit of goals so they don’t cause too much collateral damage:

  • Legal and social penalties: Laws often function like impact regularizers. You may want to maximize profit, but you can’t burn down a competitor’s shop, dump waste into a river, or bulldoze someone’s house. These rules penalize impact that’s harmful to the broader environment, even if it would make your narrow task easier.

  • Professional ethics: Doctors, engineers, and lawyers are given latitude to act, but codes of ethics restrict high-impact shortcuts. For instance, a doctor can’t test a risky drug on unwitting patients even if it might yield fast results.

  • Cultural taboos / norms: Everyday actions are shaped by informal “regularizers.” You don’t scream in a library or cut in line, even if those acts might bring you small personal benefits. The norm penalizes disproportionate disruption.

  • Divination and ritual checks (historical): In antiquity, rulers often consulted oracles or omens before major actions. In effect, this limited rash, high-impact interventions by inserting an extra “cost” before big changes could be made—an early cultural mechanism to regularize impact.

Impact regularizers for humans look like the bundle of social, legal, and moral constraints that penalize changes too disruptive to the shared environment, even when those changes would advance an individual’s immediate objective.
