Is It an Argument

Part of the lesson here is learning what it means to make an argument. Here's an example of using Claude to assess whether we are making arguments or just making assertions.

Here was our prompt: For Tuesday's class we'll have students debate a claim based on Christian's The Alignment Problem. For preclass prep students are asked to make a brief argument on both sides of a claim. Here are some examples. Evaluate these on the the basis of whether or not the student response is the basis of an argument or not and give brief formative feedback about that for each one.

Imperfect Decision-making

An important use case for AI models is making decisions in organizations (applications, adjudications, etc.).

Offer a brief argument on both sides of this claim:

“Even if a model or algorithm is biased or can be inaccurate, we should compare that to human decision-making rather than stop using the tool.”

StudentClaude
Anonymous 8nde

YES It is hard to have a hard definition of what is right and what is wrong, even with humans. Models reflect what is currently defined as right or wrong, so even if humans did the task instead, they would also do the wrong thing.

NO Unlike humans, machines do not stop what they are doing just because they are wrong. They don't have that human conscience and sense and will continuing do the wrong thing at high efficiency.

 Argument. Reasons from an epistemic premise (no hard definition of right/wrong) to a comparative conclusion (humans would err too). Argument. Warrant is machines' lack of self-correction ("don't stop just because they're wrong") — a real mechanism, not just assertion.
Anonymous 8n9jz

Yes : Models may still be more consistent, efficient, and less biased than human decision makers even if they are imperfect. 

No: Bias or errors in models can affect many people at scale. If the decisions are unfair or harmful, efficiency doesn't justify using them.

 Assertion. Same problem as 8nde1's YES — asserts the comparative advantage without saying why. Argument. Scale is doing real work here: harm compounds across many people, so efficiency doesn't offset it.
Anonymous 8nldk

YES: Models can process far more cases consistently than humans and don't suffer from fatigue, mood, or individual prejudice, so a biased model may still outperform a biased human at scale. Comparing the tool to the realistic alternative (flawed human judgment), not to an impossible ideal of perfect fairness, is the fair standard.

NO: Automating a biased process gives it a false veneer of objectivity, making the bias harder to detect, question, or hold accountable than when a human makes the same call. Scaling up bad decision making through a model also means scaling up the harm to far more people than any individual human could affect.

 Argument — strongest YES so far. Two moves: a mechanism (no fatigue/mood-driven bias) and an explicit meta-point about _what the correct comparison class is_ (realistic alternative, not a perfect ideal). Argument. Mechanism (false veneer of objectivity → harder to detect/contest) plus scale. This pair is a good model answer to hold up in class.

YES: We must remember that humans are biased so if we are able to make the model less biased, we should use the model rather than people. Even if it is imperfect, it could still help reduce bias. For example, we switched from horses to cars despite the danger of cars because cars provided a substantial benefit. Even if the algorithms are biased now, it could imrpvoe over time with more research into it. People could also use models as a tool to help reduce bias (not totally relying on it).

 Argument. Uses analogy (cars replacing horses) to support "imperfect but net-beneficial." Analogy is contestable (cars vs. algorithmic bias aren't obviously parallel) — good target for a follow-up "where does the analogy break" prompt. Argument, but three separate claims bolted together (accountability gap, perception effects, training-data mechanism) without being connected into one line of reasoning. Content is there; structure isn't.
