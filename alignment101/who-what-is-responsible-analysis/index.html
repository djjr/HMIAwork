Discussion Analysis: "Who/What is Responsible?"

Topic: "Developers are responsible for the social consequences of patterns their models learn from real-world data."

Source: Canvas discussion 1334675, 23 entries (1 deleted by author, leaving 22 usable; one pseudonym, 8nboj, appears twice — once deleted, once as a real post). No entries in this topic were flagged as pasted/AI-generated text.

Student identifiers below are the anonymized Canvas entry IDs, not names. Pseudonyms don't carry across the three discussions.

YES: Developers bear responsibility for what their models do

Three main lines of argument, plus a scattering of analogies, carry the YES case — and nearly every response uses some version of the first.

1. Control implies responsibility: developers choose the data, design, and deployment context. This is the argument almost every YES response makes: developers "choose the data and decide how the model is designed and deployed" (8n9jx), "determine the safeguards" (8n7ee), and therefore "bear responsibility for foreseeable harms even if the model 'learned' the pattern rather than being explicitly programmed with it" (8nk3p). Because they have the resources to audit and test before release, "ignorance of what a model absorbed from data isn't a valid excuse" (8nk3p) — echoed by 8nc2i, 8nda6, 8ncwq, 8nboj, 8nbcs, 8ni34, 8n91f, 8nn8g, 8nr9j, 8o09w, 8nty4, 8odwf, 8oeh7, 8oh43, 8o1tc.

2. Product-liability and professional-obligation analogies. A recurring move is to treat a model like any other manufactured product or professional service: a car manufacturer "takes responsibility for the safety of their car when they release it" and "the same logic can be applied to any developer of any models" (8o04w); the Kodak film example is cited directly — "it was the creator's fault that the technology wasn't inclusive to darker skin" (8nn8g); developers have "a moral obligation to humanity to not cause harm to others" (8ncwq). One response frames it as a teaching relationship: "just like how a teacher is responsible for what a student learns" (8odwf).

3. Only developers have the knowledge to actually intervene, so responsibility has nowhere else to sit. Several responses point out that end users structurally can't audit or understand these systems, leaving developers as the only party positioned to catch problems before deployment: lawyers, judges, and defendants "can't audit COMPAS and don't know how it works. Only the developers know how it works" (8odwf, echoing 8nk3p, 8nby7).

4. Consent and legal accountability. One response adds that the people affected "did not consent to the AI," which sharpens the developer's obligation to ensure fairness before deploying it on them (8ncwq). The most evidence-heavy response in the set (8nby7) cites specific cases to argue courts are already moving this direction: a U.S. hospital risk tool that used "future cost" as a flawed proxy for medical need, systematically underserving Black patients because of how an engineer chose to model the problem (not the data itself); the Dutch tax authority's fraud-detection system, which falsely accused 26,000 families and contributed to a government collapse; and recent U.S. legal settlements/actions (SafeRent Solutions, Mobley v. Workday) holding vendors liable under existing discrimination law.

NO: Developers didn't create the bias, and responsibility should be shared or lie elsewhere

The NO case is dominated by one argument, reinforced by several supporting lines about practicality and unintended consequences.

1. Developers reflect societal bias, they don't create it — blaming them misplaces the fault. By far the most common NO move: "it is not the developers' fault for creating models that are based on real societal patterns. Blaming developers misplaces society's responsibilities and faults and uses devs as scapegoats" (8nc2i). Variants of this appear across most NO responses: models learn from "real" data (8nda6, 8n9jx, 8nk3p, 8nbcs, 8ni34, 8nn8g, 8nr9j, 8n7ee, 8nty4, 8odwf, 8oeh7, 8oh43) — several explicitly counter the hospital-tool example above by arguing "it was society's spending that was unjust. Blaming the AI hides the societal ill" (8nby7's own NO side).

2. Responsibility should be distributed, not concentrated on developers alone. A large secondary cluster argues for shared accountability across data curators, deploying organizations, and end users: "responsibility should also fall on users and society" (8n9jx); "responsibility should be distributed across data curators, deploying institutions, and end users" (8nk3p, which draws an analogy to engineers who build planes later used destructively); "Companies are more responsible... since they give the following trainings and instruct/restrict clients' use of models" (8o1tc). A related version blames the end-user's choice to deploy: "responsibility lies with the end-customer who chooses to deploy the model despite known biases" (8n91f).

3. Practical infeasibility: no one can define "good," audit every dataset, or predict every downstream use. Several responses argue the standard YES demands is unworkable: "there's no standard for a 'socially accepted pattern' which makes it virtually impossible for the developers to prematurely correct" (8nn8g); one directly cites a named source — "Bolukbasi said that 'Sociologists can't define what is good.' How can developers know whether... their models will have 'good' or 'bad' social consequences?" (8odwf); models often rely on standard public datasets developers didn't curate themselves (8odwf); the sheer scale of internet-derived data makes full bias filtering practically impossible today (8oeh7).

4. Holding developers responsible could backfire. A sharper strand argues that liability pressure produces the wrong incentives: it could "distract them from actually improving the models" (8ncwq), push them to "suppress the measurable disparities, finding an artificial way to get around this problem rather than actually addressing it" (8nn8g), or ironically cause developers to "introduce more of their own personal bias into the model" while trying to fix it (8ncwq). One response broadens this into a free-expression argument, warning that developer liability for "negative impact" sets a "dangerous precedent that stifles innovation" (8o04w).

5. The alternative to a flawed model can be worse. The same well-researched response above also makes a distinctive rebuttal on its NO side: Kenyan lenders' use of biased mobile-data credit scoring is flawed, but "the alternative for these borrowers is no access to credit at all" — refusing to build imperfect models has victims too (8nby7).

What's notably absent or thin

  • Very few responses grapple with 8nby7's most specific rebuttal to the "just reflecting society" defense — that in the hospital-tool case, the biased proxy ("future cost" for "medical need") was itself an engineering choice, not something read directly off the data. Most NO responses continue to treat "the data was biased" and "the model design choices were biased" as the same claim, when this example shows they can come apart.
  • No response engages the question of what standard should apply if not full liability or full immunity — e.g., negligence-style liability (did developers do reasonable diligence?) versus strict liability (any harm is the developer's fault regardless of care taken). The debate is largely framed as binary.
  • The legal/regulatory angle (8nby7's case citations) is raised by only one student; no one else engages with the fact that this question is already being litigated, which could have sharpened several of the "impossible to know in advance" NO arguments.
