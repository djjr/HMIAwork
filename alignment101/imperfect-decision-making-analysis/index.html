Discussion Analysis: "Imperfect Decision-Making"

Topic: *"Even if a model or algorithm is biased or can be inaccurate, we should compare that to human decision-making rather than stop using the tool."

Source: Canvas discussion 1334673, 23 entries (1 deleted by author, leaving 22 usable). One entry (8nldk) is flagged by Canvas metadata as a pasted ChatGPT transcript — its arguments are included below but marked, since it may not reflect the student's own reasoning.

Student identifiers below are the anonymized Canvas entry IDs (e.g., 8n9jz), not names.

YES: We should compare models to human decision-making, not abandon them

Four recurring moves carry almost all of the YES arguments.

1. The comparison should be to the realistic alternative, not to a perfect standard.The single most common move is to reject an implicit demand for flawless models. Students argue that the honest baseline is "flawed human judgment," not "an impossible ideal of perfect fairness" (8nldk), and that "rejecting a flawed model demands a non-existent standard of perfection" (8nbxw). Several make the point bluntly: humans are already biased, so a model doesn't need to be perfect to be an improvement (8n9jz, 8nnft, 8nbp0, 8nsg4, 8nty9, 8o35u, 8oj1q).

2. Models offer efficiency, consistency, and scale that individual human judgment can't match.Machines "don't suffer from fatigue, mood, or individual prejudice" (8nldk) and can "process far more cases consistently" (8nldk, 8odwe). A few cite empirical examples to back this up — COMPAS processing thousands of defendants (8odwe), or a bail-reform study claiming a well-tuned model could cut jailing rates 40% while detaining fewer minorities without raising crime (8nbxw). The efficiency argument is often paired with a cost-benefit framing: even an imperfect model can produce more overall good than harm at scale (8nu9k, 8nty9).

3. Model bias is measurable and correctable in a way human bias isn't. This is the more sophisticated version of the YES case, and appears in roughly a third of responses. Because models are auditable, "we can quantify how biased it is, which gives us a starting point to improve" (8nwxe) — something with "no measurable way" to do for individual humans. Several point to models actually improving over time with more data (8nnft, 8odwe cites IBM's gender-classification error-rate fix; 8nz2l stresses that we control and can audit training data in a way we can't audit a person's judgment).

4. Models should be a tool/second opinion, not an autonomous replacement. A smaller but distinct cluster reframes the debate: the answer isn't "model vs. human" but "model and human." Treat models "like consultants" whose advice is evaluated, not obeyed (8nx7d); use them as "a unique and useful second opinion" (8no7v); combine human and model judgment so each can counteract the other's biases (8n91a, 8o0bt).

One outlier (8nde1) argues from moral relativism rather than efficiency: since there's no fixed definition of "right," a human doing the same task would also encode whatever a society currently considers correct, so models aren't uniquely blameworthy.

NO: Bias in a tool used at scale is a reason to stop, not a reason to shrug

The NO side clusters just as tightly around four ideas, several of which are direct rebuttals to the YES arguments above.

1. Efficiency is precisely what makes bias dangerous — it multiplies harm rather than justifying it. This is the mirror image of the YES "scale" argument and is the single most common NO move. A biased human affects one case at a time; a biased model "affects many people at scale" (8n9jz) and "will be rapidly implemented statewide" (8nwxe). Several frame this as the real stakes of automation: "the effects of the bias are amplified" (8oeh6), and repeated small gaps between AI and human judgment compound into "larger injustice" over many uses (8oj1q).

2. Automating a decision gives it a false veneer of objectivity that makes bias harder to catch and easier to trust blindly. Because a number or output "looks objective," people (and even downstream reviewers) stop scrutinizing it — reviewers "quickly become complacent and blindly rubber-stamp machine decisions" (8nbxw), and "as use of AI increases in frequency, humans may be subconsciously impacted by their suggestions" (8o0bt, 8oe6s). This is presented as worse than an equivalently biased human, whose bias is at least recognizable as opinion rather than "fact."

3. There's no one to hold accountable when a model causes harm. This accountability gap is raised almost as often as the scale argument. A human decision-maker "can be held liable for that mistake" (8nu9k); a model's accountability "spreads... across vendors, buyers, and clients, leaving victims unable to challenge a single responsible author" (8nbxw). Two students extend this to a demand for a higher standard for automated systems than for humans, comparable to product-safety regulation — a vaccine causing even 1% harm wouldn't be approved regardless of net benefit, so AI shouldn't get a pass either (8nu9k).

4. "Better than flawed humans" is the wrong bar — models should be held to independent ethical standards. This is the most direct rebuttal to the YES framing: several students argue that pointing to human bias as a justification is itself the problem. "Giving algorithms a pass just because humans also fail simply automates and excuses injustice" (8nsg4); "if we wouldn't consider it appropriate for a person, why should a model have that privilege" (8nbp0); "we cannot regulate them for use until they meet high moral and ethical standards" (8nx7d). The underlying claim is that comparing to a low bar (bad human judgment) legitimizes bad outcomes rather than solving them.

What's notably absent or thin

  • Almost no one directly engages with whose bias is being compared (e.g., is a biased judge in a specific jurisdiction really a fair proxy for "human decision-making" generally?).
  • Few responses grapple with the fact that the YES and NO "scale" arguments are actually the same underlying fact (models affect many people) read in opposite directions — this tension is present in the data but never named explicitly by any student.
  • Only one or two entries (8n91a, 8o0bt) raise the "automation complacency" mechanism as its own argument rather than a passing aside — this is arguably the strongest NO argument and is underdeveloped.
