Control+Oversight › Control+Oversight Musings

Alignment as Control and Oversight

As an alignment mechanism, "control and oversight" is the idea that an intelligent agent remains subject to external intervention.

It is fitting that this is the last category of alignment mechanisms that we consider in this course. Control and oversight suggest that "if/when all else fails, you can always turn it off."

But in what cosmology is there an it that needs turning off and a you that decides that and can do it?

The Halting Problem

When I was defending my undergraduate thesis, one of my committee members, Soo Bong Chae, asked me about the halting problem -- determining whether an arbitrary program and input will eventually halt. I'd read about it, but my head that day was is compiler-land; I gamely suggested that discussion might better fit in a later part of the conversation. Fortunately for me, we didn't come back to it.

In 1936–37, Alan Turing showed that the halting problem is undecidable. For our purposes, this suggests that no general procedure can certify the future behavior of every sufficiently general agent under all possible inputs and environments.

Henry Gordon Rice’s 1951 theorem generalized this result: every nontrivial semantic property of programs is undecidable. Thus, if "alignment" is formalized as a nontrivial semantic property of arbitrary agents, no universal static test can always determine in advance whether an agent will remain aligned.

This does not make all formal verification impossible; restricted systems can sometimes be proven safe. However, for open-ended agents, we should not assume we can rely on static proof alone. Architectural constraints and runtime safeguards are therefore important parts of a defense-in-depth alignment strategy.

Trusting Automation

Automation means being able to turn a machine on and having it perform its task without human intervention. When we design or engineer things, we do our best to prove that they will work as designed, but automation is still a leap of faith. Turing's and Rice's results demand humility; demand that we think of our design as an existence theorem not an impossibility theorem. Our testing may have shown that it can run automatically (an existential claim (∃)), but we have not shown that failure is impossible (a universal claim (∀)). And that's why we retain some level of control. There's always an open question: can we trust it? Trust requires being able to claim that for all runs, failure does not happen. More successful runs support the former claim but we can never prove the latter. And yet we may decide to trust.

This reminds us of Hume's "induction" problem. In his 1739 Treatise of Human Nature and 1748 Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, David Hume argued that predicting the future from the past relies on assumptions that cannot be proven. Induction extrapolates from the past but cannot guarantee its conclusion without assuming properties of the world we are testing.1

1 AI (Gemini) notes these three responses to Hume: Karl Popper rejected induction as a route to proving general laws, arguing science instead tests hypotheses through deductive falsification; Statisticians use probability theory and Bayesian updating to model how rational beliefs shift with new evidence; pragmatists treat induction as a practical tool that works in practice or view habit and cognitive heuristics as natural features of human inquiry. See also Nelson Goodman's "grue paradox."
Trusting what we automate is inductive. Ronald Reagan famously said "trust but verify." Our version will be trust, but check it.

A second midcentury result, Gödel's second theorem (1931), is also relevant. It says no sufficiently strong system can prove its own consistency. Turing, Rice, and Hume say we have to check; Gödel says the checking will need to be external.

Thus far, we have been playing in the land of theory. The pragmatists' response to Hume (and to some degree the engineers' response to non-engineers' safety concerns) is that good enough is often good enough and that imperfect formal verification can in many cases suffice. There might still be a way to build things well enough that we can just trust them.

The sociologist Charles Perrow studied plane crashes and industrial accidents. He introduced the concept of "normal accidents." All the engineering calculations and formal verification in the world, even when all the numbers work, is still just proving things about a model of the world, not the world itself. Perrow argues that in systems with complexity and tight coupling, empirically, failure is a normal feature.

About that "Cosmology"

What about that "cosmology" I asked about at the top? By cosmology I mean what are the things in the world we are implicitly describing? Where am I, the narrator standing in that world when I talk about "it failing" or "I trust it" or "I can turn it off"? I think our starting point is that there are things in the world and there are us, intelligent beings who can create and understand and use those things. I can trust the machines I build to do the things that I want them to do. And if they do not I can turn them off or destroy them. So far, in that cosmology, I am in charge.

But humans have struggled with this for a long time. We are very clever. We are clever enough to ponder our own cleverness and to "stand on the shoulders of giants" to be even more clever from generation to generation. People like Turing and Gödel were not the first to remind the rest of us that our cleverness has limits. A common trope of human finitude has been the idea that there is something over us or outside of us. In one context it's god; in another it's society; for Émile Durkheim they are the same thing.2

2 For Durkheim, God is society represented and experienced as a transcendent power. He talks about this in The Elementary Forms of Religious Life (1912), especially Book II, Chapter 7, "The Origin of These Beliefs: The Origin of the Idea of the Totemic Principle or Mana." Here Durkheim argues that the clan’s god is "the clan itself," personified through the totem. This is where he develops the link between collective gatherings, sacred power, and "collective effervescence."

In Greek tragedy the hubristic individual is the designer who believes he has seen the impossibility of the failure of his design, that he has achieved a state that stands outside and above regulation and intervention. When the all-too-clever person starts to admire their own intellect above the collective wisdom of society (or the omniscience of the gods) a line gets crossed.

It it not always in the designer where the hubris lay: Daedalus knew the limits of his design: don't fly too close to the sun. It was Icarus, the operator, whose deployment of the design caused a problem.

This reminds us that hubris has two homes. The designer may believe their design is beyond intervention; the deployed agent may believe its judgment beats that of potential overseers. The tragedy is rooted in the designer who doesn't include an off-switch and the agent that won't honor it.

This neat little cosmology - society or gods sitting outside of systems or agents - is not the end of the story. Control mechanisms are themselves just existence theorems: we don't have any proof that the controllers are not fallible (call on Juan and Jasmine for redundancy). Plato's "who guards the guards" problem can't be solved by inventing a perfect infallible overseer. So what "sits above"?

For the Greeks it's Clotho Κλωθώ (the spinner), Lachesis Λάχεσις (the allotter), and Atropos Ἄτροπος (the inevitable, the unturning, death), the Moirai or Fates who are personifications of destiny.

For us we have things like "the rule of law" or pluralistic, mutual checking. So the key to tragedy is not "the individual cannot defy the gods" but rather "nothing gets to stand outside of regulation." But Gödel's result still haunts us.

Control mechanisms are the portfolio of things we put in place to keep a lid on this conundrum. But anything we can do has its own failure mode. Off-switches create incentives to disable off-switches and checking and monitoring engenders Goodhart effects. Bainbridge's "irony of automation" (1983) - as more things are automated, humans are left in charge of what is not automated and so have progressively less practice at overseeing what is automated.3

3 Bainbridge, Lisanne (1983-11-01). "Ironies of automation". Automatica. 19 (6): 775–779. doi:10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8. ISSN 0005-1098. S2CID 12667742
Keeping control is about ongoing vigilance - use it or lose it.

Implicit in what we have said so far is that the regulator is independent of the regulatee. In Greek tragedy the gods or the demos are offstage watching the hero. In our contemporary AI world, the overseer and the overseen are the same entity: developers and AI companies oversee themselves. It's as if Prometheus gets to decide whether his gift is safe. Our musings here assume an independent enforcer that we don't yet have for AI.

There is another hubristic error in all of this. The vision of the independent enforcer is a vain attempt to escape the fundamentally recursive or dialectical nature of the situation. In both democratic society and a world where we co-exist with AI agents there is no outside place to stand. No one gets to sit in the bank office and separately "do the accounts." So we have this irony: we require "outside" checks and control but there is no simple "outside." This is the conundrum of democracy that Madison was struggling with. "Men" constitute their government in both senses of the word constitute (as came up in our conversation about constitutions - and don't miss that word itself).

The practical payoff for the cards

We now have an organizing question for Control & Oversight cards: "Because we cannot prove ( ), we retain ( )."

Mechanism What we can't prove What we retain
Kill switches that it won't go off course the ability to interrupt it
Sandboxing how it will behave in the world containment while we watch
Access control that power will be used well limits on scope
Governance structures that judgment is sound review and the power to step in
Whistleblowing that overseers see everything sensing spread across many people
When we run a machine, we delegate authority to the machine - what it can do without human intervention. Our testing and proving establishes a warrant - that which we know about the machine. Automation is delegation without real-time supervision. Delegation can be scoped to warrant, but in an open world the scope's edge is itself unwarranted territory, so a residual gap remains, located at the boundary. Control and oversight are how we handle the boundary.

An established warrant has five different ways the existential finding can fall short of a universal one:

Gap Between… Why it can't be closed
Induction runs we've seen and all runs it will face You can't test every case; deployment produces new ones
Representation the model we proved things about and the world it acts in A proof is about the map, not the territory (Perrow)
Specification what we said we wanted and what we actually want A system can provably meet the spec and still miss the intent (Goodhart, outer alignment)
Temporality when it was certified and now The world drifts, the system adapts or degrades, the license gets stale
Standing party making the claim; an independent verifier Does the prover have an interest in the answer
Representation and Temporality are implicated in robustness to domain shift. Induction somewhat related.
Specification is about objective function specification as in reward hacking.
Safe exploration
Side effects have a bit of representation and a bit of induction
Scalable oversight is what we need when delegation exceeds warrant.

Another Look at Our Cards

I turn on the sprinkling system. I hire a financial advisor. We task the marketing department with a new product launch. I give my Claudebot control of vacation planning. When we delegate continuous autonomous operation but we have only warranted good behavior in certain test conditions, the gap is inductive, temporal, and representational as we assume it will stay on course in all conditions. A kill switch covers the gap: we have the right to stop it without needing to prove a fault.

We hire a CEO or a Sometimes we give a system discretion of consequential decisions based on competence at the time of appointment. We assume judgments will continue to serve the mission. Specification and temporal aspects of warrant. Governance structures allow us to review and override.

As a society we allow organizations to act like artificial persons and within organizations there are official chains of oversight (scalable oversight is the relevant concrete problem). We delegate insofar as we trust the official chain of oversight. The we here is "society" outside the organization. What is warranted is the designed and tested oversight channels as they exist. The gap is between what overseers can see and will report honestly and what we are trusting to be overseen (partly a standing issue with hints of inductive - assume see something say something continues; representational - assume "supervise" in theory matches practice; specification - assume we have directed managers correctly; temporal - assume no drift, no normalization of deviance, etc.). By supporting or encouraging whistleblowing we have our deviance sensors set to not only work in the chain of command that might attenuate them.

With sandboxing we are basically NOT delegating. What's supposedly warranted is that the sandbox is a consequence-free zone. There is a representational problem in that we don't know if sandbox behavior carries over. There might be an inductive problem insofar as we assume that what happens in the sandbox stays in the sandbox. Obvious connection here to safe exploration.

Most intelligence is more general than what we want to use it for. Friends and colleagues are full scale people even though we relate to them only in parts. Organizations are powerful tools that can do things beyond what their mission or charter specify. Experts can be polymaths. On the one hand, we explicitly delegate to these other intelligences that which we want them to do. On the other hand, we implicitly delegate all that we don't explicitly forbid. The warrant is competence that's been shown and relevance to us that's been confirmed. The gap is between what they can do and what they should do. We can close that gap by explicit non-delegation.

So the control and oversight mechanisms address alignment by two different approaches to the gap. Kill switches, governance structures, and whistleblowing acknowledge delegation exceeds warrant and try to cover the gap. Sandboxing and access control pull the reins back on delegation to trim the gap. Close to these are qualification (apprenticeship, credentialed privileging, limit amount of delegation; certification and licensing limit delegation to agents fully warranted.

The relationship between delegation and warrant can be expressed in terms of scope. What is warranted defines the scope of situations in which delegation is acceptable. But then the question becomes how to detect when we have left the scope (how does the autonomous vehicle decide the weather is bad? - that's now a new capability).

Automation is delegation without real-time supervision. Delegation can be scoped to warrant, but in an open world the scope's edge is itself unwarranted territory, so a residual gap remains, located at the boundary.

Control is the price of delegating more than you can vouch for.
