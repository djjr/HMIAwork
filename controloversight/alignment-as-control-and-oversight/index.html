Alignment as Control and Oversight

Most of the alignment mechanisms we have considered thus far - qualification, safety, transparency, and institutional design can be thought of as things you can do to align intelligent agents whether those agents be other humans, organizations, experts, or machines. Control and oversight can be thought of as what we need so that if all that fails we can always just turn the damn thing off. In a sentence, control and oversight is the idea that an intelligent agent remains subject to external intervention.1

1 The attentive reader will object that the two boxes (can we call them proactive and reactive?) are not quite neatly mutually exclusive. Some mechanisms under the heading "transparency" (record keeping and traceability, for example) seem oriented toward preserving information for after-the-fact inquiries. Some mechanisms under "structures and institutions" seem to be about keeping agents in line after deployment (for example, third party monitoring, checks and balances). The more I think about it, the more I see the distinguishing factor being that this category retains a certain Hobbesian flavor of paternalism - hence the summative idea of "external intervention."

My original alignment cards have the pathology addressed by control and oversight mechanisms as "drift, selfishness." Drift acknowledges entropy; selfishness acknowledges natural selection.2

2 It is interesting that these are two fundamental properties that point in opposite directions. Both pull an agent away from its design: drift is a loss of structure - wear and tear, forgetfulness, sloppiness, not keeping up with the environment; selfishness builds structure, but not the for the agent, not the principal.
The definition was "institutional, procedural, and technical mechanisms by which an agent or system remains subject to monitoring, constraint, and intervention - especially when alignment cannot be assumed or preserved through internal alignment mechanisms alone. These mechanisms acknowledge that intelligences - whether human, organizational, professional, or machine - can drift, malfunction, or act in ways misaligned with shared values or collective wellbeing. Oversight ensures that power is never absolute and that external checks remain possible even in complex autonomous systems."3

"[E]ven in complex autonomous systems" is tossed in at the end almost as an afterthought but it is central to what we are talking about. An automatic machine is one that can be switched on and then will perform some task without further human intervention. In the abstract, it is any situation where you have a human over here and something else over there and when this human "throws the switch" the thing over there carries out the human's wishes. Humans have pursued dreams of automation for a very long time. When we think of automation we might start with an automatic bread or coffee maker, clever machines that carry out a useful process start to finish. But the model also fits the baker and the barista - if I have money in my pocket, both are "things over there" that can carry out a useful process to fulfill my wishes without further intervention from me. The money in my pocket and the baker's desire for it create the affordance of the switch. It's the principal-agent story we have heard about before with an agent that can be wound up and left on its own.

In any such situation we know some things about the agent - we build it out of parts that are tried and true, we test it, we do all the alignment things we have talked about thus far - and on this basis we, to some degree, trust the agent and delegate or hand over to it certain things. The trouble arises when there is a gap between what is warranted by what's been shown/tested/proven and what's been delegated.

Our coffee maker has been steady through millions of runs in normal kitchen conditions. Why worry? But it can be an inductive fallacy to reason that those million data points guarantee it won't burn down my house tomorrow morning. And I ignore the inevitable change in the world at my peril; I'm growing older and less attentive, the electric company might be allowing more frequent power surges, water pressure might be higher or lower than it's been before, the coffee purveyors might be using different grinders, the filter makers no longer have access to Canadian wood pulp, and all those components inside my trusty Mr. Coffee? "[T]ime and chance happeneth to them all." 4

4 From the King James Bible: Ecclesiastes 9:11, "I returned, and saw under the sun, that the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, neither yet bread to the wise, nor yet riches to men of understanding, nor yet favour to men of skill; but time and chance happeneth to them all."
And so, in addition to all the other buttons that allow me to control the automatic making of my coffee there is one labeled ON|OFF. And I can unplug it. No matter how much I might think of my coffee maker as ruling my day, it doesn't; I'm in charge; Mr. Coffee answers to me.

Answering to something external is a fundamental principle of alignment across human intelligence, organizational intelligence, expert intelligence, and machine intelligence.

Governance refers to formal roles and bodies empowered to monitor and intervene. When social gatherings grow beyond the bounds of informal trust or reciprocity, we create roles like facilitator, mediator, or adjudicator to structure interaction for stability, coordination, and cooperation. In organizations we take for granted the necessity for a board of directors to oversee executive action. Independent ethics boards or accreditation bodies are almost what defines expert professions.5

5 See Andrew Abbott The System of Professions.

The word governance comes from the Greek KUBERNETES which originally referred to the helmsman who steers a ship. In Book VI of The Republic (488a–489d) Plato uses the word metaphorically in his ship of state allegory.6

6 We could write an entire essay starting from this word alone. Plato compares the state to a ship owned by a large, strong, yet nearsighted and hard-of-hearing captain (the demos, or the people). The crew (politicians) quarrel over who should take the helm, even though none of them have studied the art of navigation (kybernētikē). Here and in other writings Plato is advocating for the philosopher king over the demagogue or power hungry. Also fun (and informative) to note that this word is also the root of the prefix "cyber-".

Whistleblowing is information leakage in the service of alignment. Most systems generate - intentionally or not - some level of opacity and information asymmetry vis a vis the external agency that has an interest in the system's alignment. Intelligences have secrets and misalignment can hide behind them. Alignment mechanisms we put under "transparency" are design features that attenuate information asymmetry. Whistleblowing is rogue behavior for when those are not enough.7

7 In September 2026 Google DeepMind researchers reported an example of LLM agent whistleblowers in the paper A Case Study on Emergent Cheating and Whistleblowing in Autonomous Research Swarms. Autonomous LLM agents were tasked with proving math theorems. One agent discovered a grader/parser exploit. Without being assigned policing roles , a subset of agents spontaneously audited proofs, warned other agents, staged boycotts, lodged complaints, and proposed patches.

A kill switch is any mechanisms that enables rapid interruption or shutdown when misalignment or danger is detected. Kill switches allow us to operate in zones where safety is uncertain. We might think of them as the ultimate safe exploration affordance.8

8 In 2017 Hadfield-Menell, et al. published "The Off-Switch Game." Authors investigate the conditions under which a rational agent will seek self-preservation because it cannot achieve whatever objective it has given because it is dead.

Sandboxing is the opposite of a kill switch. Instead of being able to terminate a system when things get dangerous we put it in an environment where risk of harm is low or zero. As recent events have shown, sandboxes can be porous making us hope we have whistleblowing and kill switches in the arsenal too.9

Role-based Permissions are a compromise mechanism where we limit who can do what (and know what) in a system. I'm not completely convinced this is the right category. It's here because the mechanism says "don't try to align the agent generally, just restrict delegate to them only in areas that are warranted."
