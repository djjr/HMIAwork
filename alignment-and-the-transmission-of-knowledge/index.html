Alignment and the Transmission of Knowledge.md

The Master's Address to the Guild

(a hall in a university town, perhaps 1450)

Masters and fellows: why do we teach at all? Because we are mortal and what we know is not. So ask the first question: what is good teaching? Every master here will answer the same — it is the teaching that keeps the knowledge true not merely in your student, but in your student's student, and in scholars whose faces you will never see. We do not teach a man. We teach a chain.

And on the making of chains, look what we have wrought. A boy enters this hall knowing nothing, and in seven years he carries Aristotle, Galen, and the computus of the heavens — learning that took mankind forty generations to gather, poured into him in one. Each master dies; the faculty does not. Each manuscript crumbles; the text survives in its copies. Imagine, if you can bear it, what men who inherit everything might add. There is no limit to where such a chain can reach.

But I wish to point at a problem, and it lies in the link — in the passage from master to student, which none but master and student ever witness.

When I test my student, I ask whether he knows what I know. But I taught him — I set the questions, I judge the answers, and my own standing rises with his success. The father grades his own son and calls it a proof. And I can examine only what he holds today; I cannot examine what he will pass on when I am dust. A copyist's error is at least on the page, where a later hand may find it. A teaching error is copied into a living man, who copies it into others, each copy certifying the next. In a single scholar, a mistake is a mistake. In a lineage, a mistake becomes an inheritance.

And nothing need be wicked for this to happen. Each of us teaches most warmly what we ourselves discovered, trims what we never quite mastered, smooths the doubts that trouble our own doctrines — and each student receives the trimmed thing as the whole thing, and trims again. The chain does not break. It bends, link by link, each link true to the one before it and all of them together facing away from where they began.

Some of you say it will never come to that — the texts are our anchor; a man may drift but the page does not. To you I say: go into the libraries and collate. I have set two copies of the same Galen side by side, one from Bologna's line and one from Paris's, and they disagree — not as a blunder disagrees, but as two honest memories of the same grandfather disagree, each certain, each certified. And each line, shown the other's copy, says "yours is the corrupt one." The page drifts wherever only its own children are permitted to check it. We must be careful with "never."

And let no one say the remedy is to stop the teaching. Knowledge unhanded dies with its holder; the plague years showed us whole arts going into the ground. The chain is not the danger — the unwatched chain is. Think of the aqueduct: the Romans moved a river a hundred miles and lost barely a drop, because at every arch stood an inspection, and every span was measured against the level, which does not flatter and cannot be taught to. We must build for knowledge what they built for the water.

So I propose, and propose we bind ourselves in statute:

First: no line shall be the only line. Every art taught here shall be taught in at least one other faculty, founded apart from us, owing us nothing — and where their doctrine and ours divide, the division shall be published, not buried. Where independent chains agree, the agreement came from the source; where they differ, we have found a bend. But guard against the counterfeit: two faculties that share all their masters are one line wearing two hoods.

Second: the examiner shall not be the teacher. Let students be tried by masters of the rival line, who gain nothing by their passing. It is uncomfortable. It is meant to be. Comfort is what the bend feels like from inside.

Third: every generation returns to the sources. Not the gloss upon the gloss, but the oldest witnesses we hold — each cohort collating anew and publishing what they find, so the chain is measured against its beginning and not merely its latest link. A chain that must face its origin cannot bend far in secret.

Fourth: each master shall register his departures. Where you teach other than you were taught, say so in the faculty's book — not to forbid the change, for the chain must grow or it is a fossil, and half our inheritance is corrections. But let change travel labeled, so our successors can tell the inheritance from the amendment.

And last: the chain shall keep a record the chain cannot amend. What is sealed in the common library, no later hand may improve — for the mending hand and the bending hand cannot be told apart at fifty years' distance. A lineage that can revise its past can never be corrected by it.

You will object that this is a great withdrawal of trust among brothers. It is not the men in this hall I distrust. It is every man at fifty years' distance, myself first of all — my errors will outlive my power to catch them, and they will be taught by men who love me. These statutes are not a wall against wicked masters. They are a rail against honest ones, on a road that bends so slowly no walker feels the turning.

Hear my claim: links that cannot check themselves can still be forged into a chain that checks itself — line against line, generation against source, the record against the reciters. The same craft that carries knowledge down the years can carry the correction of knowledge down the years, if we build it in now, while we are still the founders and not yet the founded-upon. And that forging is the most important work this guild can do for the next fifty years — and for all the years after, because of all works it is the one that can never once stay finished.
