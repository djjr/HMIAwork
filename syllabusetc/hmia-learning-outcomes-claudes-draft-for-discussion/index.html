#learning-outcome-index

High-level families of learning outcomes, swept from the syllabus, module overviews, and session materials. Each family would decompose into 2–6 individual LO files. Families are not the same as modules — several cut across the whole course. They are candidates for the viewer's "cluster" dimension, or the LO-file naming rubric, or both.

1. AlignmentConcept — Command of the core concept

Own the concept of alignment itself: process vs. state definitions; the three-level gap (does what I said / what I meant / what I should have meant); why alignment is hard (impossibility of full specification, Goodhart's law and proxy-gaming, competence–intent independence); vocabulary (agent, objective, proxy, specification, optimization); alignment vs. safety vs. ethics vs. capabilities; misconception-clearing (misalignment is mundane and structural, not robots turning evil). Source: Module 1 (Alignment 101), Christian, Krakovna, Hendrycks.

2. FourProblems — The four alignment problems as a framework

State, distinguish, and illustrate the four alignment problems — human, organizational, expert, machine — and articulate what each shares with and how each differs from the others. Includes the signature claim of the course: being able to argue cogently that "intelligence alignment is not a new problem." Source: Module 2; the 7-minute oral-exam objective.

3. AnalogicalMethod — Analogical reasoning as a disciplined tool

Use analogy deliberately and critically: map structure from a familiar domain (human alignment solutions) to an unfamiliar one (AI), identify where an analogy carries and where it breaks, and avoid seductive but false parallels. This is the course's methodological engine and deserves its own outcomes rather than living implicitly inside others. Source: Module 1 (Thinking Analogically), used throughout.

4. SocialOrderScience — Social science as alignment science

Understand the problem of social order as humanity's original alignment research program: why individual rationality can produce collective irrationality; the nonrational foundations of cooperation (ritual, emotion, shared meaning); how social theory (Hobbes, Collins, Hechter & Horne) frames coordination and cooperation. Source: Module 3 framing sessions; Social Science Bootcamp.

5. EthicsInPerspective — Ethics relocated, not rejected

Explain why "build in the values" is one alignment mechanism among many; why principles alone are not enough; and engage the "aligned to whom?" question — whose values, aggregated how, decided by whom (Gabriel's value-alignment work). This carries the FA5 ethical-reflection requirement without being an ethics course. Source: "Isn't It All Just Ethics?", "Principles Are Not Enough", Module 1 Ethics 101.

6. MechanismRepertoire — The catalog of alignment mechanisms

Identify, explain, and give concrete examples of the human alignment mechanisms the course surveys: shared meaning, hierarchy, markets, groups; traits and qualification; deterrence, incentives, control & oversight, transparency & record-keeping, structures & institutional design, safety engineering. Probably the largest family; each mechanism plausibly gets its own LO (mirroring how each has its own session). Source: Modules 3 and 4.

7. TransferToAI — Mapping mechanisms onto machine alignment

Take a human alignment mechanism and evaluate its machine-intelligence analog: what does "hierarchy" or "licensure" or "audit" look like for AI systems, what transfers, what fails, and what's genuinely novel about the machine case. This is where families 3 and 6 pay off — the course's synthetic move. Source: threaded through Modules 2–4; Wiener session; alignment decks.

8. CaseAnalysis — Structured analysis of alignment scenarios

Apply the context–perspectives–principles–consequences–course-of-action framework to concrete scenarios (COMPAS, specification gaming, principal–agent failures) across all four problem types. This is the course's stated analytic skill objective and the backbone of the Personal Alignment Decks. Source: course objectives; COMPAS session; the CARDS material.

9. PractitionerCraft — Reflective-practitioner skills

The transferable craft outcomes: read a research paper and extract insight without full technical mastery ("How to Read Hard Stuff"); speak competently and compactly to a thesis; participate in the contemporary AI-alignment conversation as a reflective engineer rather than a spectator. Source: course objectives; Module 1; oral exam.

Open questions

  1. Families 1–5 are mostly conceptual/framework outcomes; 6–7 are content-catalog outcomes; 8–9 are skill outcomes. Is that three-way texture worth encoding (e.g., as a frontmatter field), or noise?
  2. MechanismRepertoire could be one family with ~11 LOs, or split (social-foundational mechanisms from Module 3 vs. formal/institutional mechanisms from Module 4). The Module 3/4 boundary may or may not be a real conceptual boundary.
  3. TransferToAI could be a family of its own (as here) or a dimension of every mechanism LO ("…and assess its machine analog" appended to each). Separate family keeps mechanism LOs clean; embedded keeps the analogy front-and-center everywhere.
  4. Candidate viewer mapping: course = module (already provisional in _README), clusters = these families. That gives 4 courses × 9 clusters, comparable in shape to SGP's 9 × 8.
