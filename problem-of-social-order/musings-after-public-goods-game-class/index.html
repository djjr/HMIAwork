Problem Of Social Order › Musings After Public Goods Game Class

Dan Ryan October 2026

In our recent class "the problem of social order," we ran a simulation of the standard public goods game. We worked in small groups with participants getting a fixed endowment each round and getting to decide how much to contribute to a pot which would generate a proportional surplus and all would share equally in the resulting "public good." We played three phases. In phase A contributions were secret - no one knew who had donated how much. In phase B donations were visible to all and in phase C participants had the opportunity to invest in punishing others (for example those who gave too little).

Our ostensible goal here is to build a model of society. The parameters of the model are the rules of the game, the logistics, and the numbers we use for the return on public goods investment, the cost of punishing, and the impact of being punished. That's the doing of social science.

A second take is also possible. One might think about human societies as analogs of machine learning models. Society is an irregular network of intelligent nodes that behave in ways that partially depend on the world around them (which includes other agents). The settings of the game are institutions. The policies of the agents vis a vis those settings are "culture" and learned psychology. If we assume (as our public goods game does) that coordination (e.g., somebody but not everybody investing in punishment) and cooperation (e.g., maximizing donations) increase collective welfare then the question becomes how do these agents discover institutions and learn policies that improve their collective welfare?

An important observation here: over the three phases of the simulation YOU did not get to choose secrecy, visibility, or sanctioning. These were imposed by me, an EXOGENOUS designer. By contrast, in the second take above, I'm suggesting a two-timescale picture. A fast inner loop is agents adjusting their contribution (and, in mode C, sanctioning) policies. But there is also a slower, outer, loop where the agents might change the institutions (changing the mode, adjusting the sanctioning rules, etc.). This outer loop can proceed in different ways. The political scientist Elinor Ostrom describes how communities deliberately design institutions (such as fisheries management schemes) to govern the commons. Evolutionary theorists like Boyd and Richerson show how similar outcomes can result from cultural group selection (groups that land on better rules do better).

Researchers have looked into how this sort of game plays out when groups can talk and choose their sanctioning rules. Gürerk, et al. 2006 suggest being able to choose a sanctioning regime increases competitive advantage. Ostrom, Walker, and Gardner (1992) suggests that communication alone raises cooperation a lot (communication itself is an achievement of cooperation and coordination, of course, so we return to Durkheim and Collins' question about the pre-contractual basis of contract). Their point is also relevant when we recognize that both existence of punishment mechanism and willingness to punish are public goods too. There's a pre-public good basis of public good production.

What happens when agents can sanction other agents? In ML terms, this is allowing agents to rewrite each other's reward functions. In our phase C, each agent can reshape other's payoff landscape. We have a concept, "reward shaping" that is what we do as designers when we specify rewards and penalties that make a policy easier to learn with feedback along the way. Our sanctioning might be seen very loosely as reward shaping by other agents.1

1 Doesn't quite work because our agents are actually changing the other agents' optimal policy. Pioneered by Ng, Harada, and Russell (1999), potential-based reward shaping guarantees that adding a valid potential-based shaping function F(s, a, s') = γ φ(s') - φ(s) preserves the exact set of optimal policies as the original reward function.

There are connections here to a few of our generic alignment tools. The visibility that we introduced in phase B is a form of transparency. There are hints of interpretability here - how much can we infer about how other agents are approaching the process based on what we can observe? A sort of proto-reputation as an alignment mechanism is at work insofar as players are keeping track of others - if we allowed agents to communicate we see reputation and gossip being potential factors.

Disanalogy

My suggestion above that we might see society as an ML model is off the mark in a few ways. A single model has a single loss function and a single gradient. Society doesn't. Each person is a (at least partially) separate optimizer and there is no agent optimizing aggregate welfare. So, the better analogy is to a multi-agent learning system where there is no shared objective (although somewhat correlated individual objectives). The interesting question is then whether and how a latent collective objective finds its way "inside" the individual agents.

We have also grossly oversimplified things by providing such a simple welfare function. In real societies that's hidden and contested. We meet a piece of this in the ethics module when we ask "whose values?"

Is Punishment Always Beneficial?

A few groups made the excellent observation that in the aggregate they seemed to do worse in phase C. Researchers have found similar results. Herrmann et al. (2008) found that people sometimes engage in "anti-social" sanctioning, punishing high contributors. In the same setup we used, sometimes net earnings decrease under a sanctioning regime because punishment destroys resources. On the other side, Hermann et al. found lots of cross-cultural variation and Gächter, Renner, and Sefton (2008) found that the losses from punishment go away when the game goes on longer.

Parting Question

Suppose you could have voted on or debated the rules for a next phase. What do you think you would have recommended and why? How would you group have chosen?
