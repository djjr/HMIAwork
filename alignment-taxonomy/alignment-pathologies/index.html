Alignment Taxonomy › Alignment Pathologies

Mechanism Category: Qualification. Pathologies are incompetence and trust. How does one agent know that another agent CAN do what it will be asked to do?

Two works by economists are relevant. Akerlof's "Market for Lemons" (1970) is about adverse selection where you cannot verify quality ex ante so you have to trust a signal that can be gamed. A M Spence's market signaling is parallel.

1. The credentialing-instrument cluster — Credentials, Licensing, Registration, Certification. They differ on enforcement stringency. Licensing is state-backed and exclusionary (practice without it is illegal). Certification is voluntary market signaling (you can practice without it, it just tells buyers something). Registration is the lightest touch — just a listing, often no gatekeeping at all. Abbott's The System of Professions (1988) argued that professions compete by escalating along this axis (registration → certification → licensing) to lock out competitors.

2. The integrity-vetting cluster — Security Clearance & Background Vetting, Character & Fitness/Conflict of Interest Attestation, Reference checks. Not for ability but benevolence and integrity. Worth naming that split explicitly to students: a bar exam tests whether you can do the job; a character-and-fitness review tests whether you'll do it honestly. Screening isn't just about competence, institutions also try to buy integrity insurance up front rather than relying entirely on control to catch it later. A design choice.

3. The graduated-trust cluster — Apprenticeship/Supervised Practice, Provisional/Probationary License, Credentialed Privileging. Not binary in/out gate but narrowing supervision as demonstrated competence accumulates. Cf. staged autonomy in AI deployment: canary releases, capability evals gating tool access, human-in-the-loop that's progressively relaxed.

4. Continuous monitoring and Log books — flag, don't file yet. These aren't pre-hoc qualification so they seem a bit like record keeping or control. But in some contexts, e.g., airline pilot requirements aren't "lifetime hours logged," they're rolling windows (e.g., 3 takeoffs/landings in the last 90 days to carry passengers). Stop flying and you become unqualified. The log is constitutive is the qualification, decaying in real time whether or not anything ever went wrong. By contrast, a notary must keep a log but missing an entry is a compliance failure. The log is evidentiary.

A surgeon's case log (does volume-in-procedure lapse count against privileging?) or a therapist's supervised-hours log during licensure are constitutive; an auditor's engagement log or a chain-of-custody log are evidentiary.

For AI transfer we might expect a "type rating" as a scoped capability claim as a qualified on this airframe, not airplanes-in-general. That maps onto capability evals scoped to a specific deployment context (this tool access, this model version) rather than a global "is it aligned" claim, and it inherits the decay problem too — a model fine-tuned or an underlying checkpoint updated should arguably require re-currency, not grandfather forward on a stale eval, same as a pilot who hasn't flown the type in 90 days doesn't get to lean on last year's log entries.
