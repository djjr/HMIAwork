On Creative Listening

*tl;dr: In collaborative settings most participants are too busy composing their contributions and competing for the floor to listen deeply to what others are saying. The ostensible goal of collaboration - my ideas building on yours, yours heading in new directions because of mine - is undermined by the way we structure the process. A few simple principled reforms can make a difference.

One of my assignments at both Iovine Young Academy and University of Toronto was to teach design thinking in an way that made its practical insights transformative in contexts outside design proper.

One dimension of this was looking critically at collaborative processes and workflows - how can you build a process that maximizes benefit a team can extract from its members (and alternatively, how can you facilitate maximal contribution from each member). I describe a piece of this as "creative listening." Premise is that we need structures to help us strike the right balance between how much of our cognitive bandwidth we devote to what we want to say and how much we devote to taking in what others have to say. My hypothesis is that status quo habits privilege the former at around 90-95%.

Components of training include high investment in quality of questions you pose to a team; explicit alternation between heads-down, heads-together, and whole room activity; strict timing; writing ideas down and reading what one has written down; sequential turn taking; deploying structured prompting (e.g., yes, and; why and because; Mad-Libs).

  • Heads-down, heads-together, and room-together. Make explicit time to think alone because you want independently sourced ideas in the mix before group think and perceived consensus kicks in. Heads together (e.g., pairs) because you want ideas to encounter small scale diversity and sharpening via having to get one other mind to understand them. Three of four heads together gives benefit of audience hearing and responding and allows floor-time-per-person:total-time ratio to be optimized AND at many stages of the process the whole room doesn't need to hear every idea. Deliberately script of heads-down and -together and room-together to serve the collaborative purpose at hand.
  • Strict timing. Value of time is linear in the number of participants; the wasting of time can be quadratic in the number of participants. Move things at a rapid pace; fast-pace is unnerving at first but can help people focus as they become accustomed; balance diminishing returns to more time spent on a task; "enforce" phases (now turn to question 2); remind people to use all the available time (chide those who finish their turn early and go silent - you have an intelligent audience, USE IT!)
  • Writing ideas down and reading what one has written down. Writing forces team members to think through what they are thinking and to make choices in how to articulate it. Writing down what you know you will read fosters commitment to ideas. Reading what you wrote down means that you can listen to what others say rather than composing and recomposing and reconsidering what you will say when others are talking. Reading what you wrote helps discipline team members toward respecting divided shared time.
  • Sequential turn taking. Everyone gets heard. Everyone knows they will be heard and when their turn will come. No cognitive bandwidth is wasted trying to get the floor and no emotional bandwidth is wasted worrying about whether one will get the floor.
  • Deploy structured prompting (e.g., yes, and; why and because; Mad-Libs). Creativity is born of constraint. Prompts encourage facilitator or teacher to think harder about what one is trying to elicit. Structured responses are easier for participants to stack next to each other as they hear from others. Facilitates collection of "what the team said" as data for slicing and dicing.
