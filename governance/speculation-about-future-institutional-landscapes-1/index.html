Governance › Speculation about Future Institutional Landscapes 1

*Back and forth with Claude and ChatGPT influenced by my (DJR) thinking about human scale and democratic institutions.

When we base our work mostly on existing research we end up biased away from "remaking democratic institutions for a world with AGI." What if we grant ourselves license to do some disciplined speculation. Democratic institutions are already a partial solution to a competence-asymmetry problem at the heart of 'governing the AI transition': citizens can't evaluate policy directly and governance doesn't have a direct line to individual and group preferences, so the system has evolved workarounds. Such as:

  • Judging outcomes. Voters assess results rather than methods.
  • Replacing agents. Officials who fail can be removed.
  • Adversarial procedure. Courts and the press test claims so lay people don't have to.
  • Long time frame public conversations. Deliberation is highly decentralized with multiple opportunities for diffusion and information percolation and time for different constituencies to "catch up" at different rates.
  • Economic leverage. Governments need citizens' labor, taxes, and military service, which forces them to listen.
  • DJR: what else? What are the underlying dimensions here? Is this the right angle for viewing institutions as object of AGI stresses?

AGI stresses each of these differently. So instead of asking "what new institutions?", we can ask which mechanism breaks and what would repair it. The roles fall out of the repairs.

1. Outcome legibility breaks: build adversarial translation

When the relevant decisions concern systems nobody can inspect, voters and legislators can't judge outcomes either. Courts have long handled this by not requiring the judge to be an expert. Instead, two opposing experts argue in front of a lay decider. That maps closely onto the AI-safety-via-debate idea, and it suggests a family of institutions:

  • Citizen panels with investigative power. Standing sortition bodies, something like grand juries, could review frontier deployment decisions, with red and blue teams arguing before them.
  • Adversarial briefers. A new profession trained to argue both sides of a capability or safety claim to a lay audience. It's closer to a barrister than to an auditor.
  • Panel counsel. Independent staff who help jurors ask good questions without steering the answers.

The design problem is that whoever frames the debate shapes the verdict. So the framing function itself would need rotation, contestation, or its own review.

2. The speed mismatch: legislate conditions, not rules

Legislatures work in years and capabilities move in months. One repair is conditional legislation: statutes written as if-then triggers ("if a system demonstrates capability X, obligations Y attach automatically"), paired with an independent body that certifies when a trigger has fired. Amodei's "checkpoint" idea gestures at this. The democratic move is to have the legislature, not a lab or a standards body, set the thresholds.

A second repair borrows from central banking. An insulated agency gets a narrow statutory mandate plus heavy transparency obligations, the way the Fed has a legislated mandate but independent operations. Roles that follow include threshold certifiers, trigger adjudicators who settle disputes over whether a threshold has been crossed, and a public "minutes" function that explains each decision afterward.

3. The administrative state becomes the model: public model specifications

If agencies increasingly run on AI agents, then administrative discretion (which currently derives legitimacy from the election of the executive and legislative and judicial deference to expertise) lives inside model specifications and system prompts. Documents like Anthropic's constitution or OpenAI's model spec start to function as quasi-legislation. That suggests several things:

  • Specifications as public law. Specs for government-deployed models would go through something like notice-and-comment, perhaps ratification.
  • Specification counsel. A drafting profession, like legislative counsel today, that translates statutory intent into model-governing language and flags where the two diverge.
  • Interpretive tribunals. When an automated agency applies its spec to a novel case, someone has to decide what the spec meant. That is essentially administrative law for models.
  • Algorithmic ombudspersons. Officials with real model access and the power to reverse automated decisions, backed by a citizen right to appeal to a human.

4. The slow conversation compresses: protect the tempo and plurality of deliberation

This workaround depends on three properties: time, decentralization, and uneven catch-up. The public conversation happens over years. It runs through many independent channels - discourse-institutions ranging from universities and think tanks to journalistic media, to voluntary associations. Borders and languages and other obstacles to communication may actually help the planet with idea-diversity. Cf Sewall Wright's shifting balance theory suggesting that semi-isolated populations maximize evolution's capacity to meet the moment. Different constituencies arrive at understanding at their own pace, and the system waits, roughly, for enough of them to get there.

AGI pressures all three.

  • Time. Decisions arrive faster than understanding can spread through the public.
  • Decentralization. If most people form their views by consulting the same few models, the thousand channels collapse into a handful of chokepoints. Big losses possible in the ideo-diversity of a world with libraries, universities, borders, languages, local media, books, conferences, etc. Deliberation may appear decentralized while becoming centralized underneath.
  • Catch-up. Every constituency gets an instant, fluent explanation. That feels like catching up, but it may skip the slow processing through which people actually make a view their own.

There is interesting two edge sword here. AI could make catch-up faster and more equal, and tools like Polis or DeepMind's Habermas Machine suggest deliberation can be mapped and synthesized at scale. The question is whether AI accelerates the conversation or replaces it.

Possible repairs:

  • Irreversibility-weighted pacing. Reversible decisions move fast. Irreversible ones, such as deploying a system that can't be recalled or entrenching an institution, carry mandatory deliberation intervals. Only a narrow class of decisions gets slowed, but that class is protected.
  • Readiness indicators. A public statistics function, like the one that produces unemployment and inflation figures, would measure whether understanding of a major issue has actually spread across constituencies before binding decisions are made. The role here is something like a statistician of public understanding.
  • Epistemic plurality requirements. Antitrust applied to civic epistemics: diversity mandates for the models that mediate public information, plus auditors who track how concentrated the sources of opinion formation have become.
  • Catch-up infrastructure. Publicly provided explanatory tools that meet each constituency where it is, run by civic translators whose job is to help people understand, not to persuade them. A strict separation between explaining and advocating would be the core professional ethic.
  • Protected human-only spaces. Some deliberative settings, such as citizen assemblies or town meetings, would be kept free of AI synthesis. The point isn't that AI synthesis is bad. It's that preference formation needs somewhere it can happen unmediated.

This workaround is in tension with the speed-mismatch repair from before. That section tried to make governance faster, and this one tries to protect slowness. Conditional legislation may resolve it: the slow conversation happens in advance, deciding which thresholds should trigger which obligations, and only the execution runs at machine speed. Deliberation moves upstream of the crisis rather than being eliminated by it.

5. Citizens' agents become participants: a fiduciary layer

Soon many people will act through AI agents: filing comments, contacting representatives, negotiating with agencies. This creates two problems. One is loyalty: is my agent serving me, its vendor, or someone paying to shape its outputs? The other is flooding: notice-and-comment and petition systems collapse when millions of agents can generate input.

Possible repairs:

  • Fiduciary duties for personal agents. Loyalty obligations on the agents themselves, with auditors who certify that loyalty. This is the lawyer-client analogy applied to software.
  • A publicly provisioned civic agent. Something like the public defender, so that people without resources still have a competent advocate in dealing with government.
  • Personhood-weighted participation. Civic channels where input is tied to verified individuals, so influence can't be multiplied by running more agents.

6. The leverage problem

Democratic concessions have historically tracked how much states needed their citizens as workers, taxpayers, and soldiers. If AGI weakens that dependence, the underlying bargaining power erodes even if every formal institution stays intact. Some have called this the "intelligence curse," after the resource curse in petrostates, where governments that don't need citizens' taxes stop answering to them.

Repairs here are structural rather than procedural:

  • Citizen ownership of AI returns. Sovereign-wealth-style funds or public equity stakes in frontier labs, so citizens keep an economic stake that doesn't depend on their labor.
  • Public compute. Treating compute as a public utility, so that capability isn't monopolized.
  • Anti-concentration rules. Constitutional-level limits on concentrating AI capability in the state, not just in firms.

DJR want to add Hirschman's Exit, Voice, and Loyalty to this. Hirschman's idea is that an organization (firm, a party, or a state) in decline, survives only with feedback. Members provide feedback by leaving (exit) or complaining (voice). The leverage problem is about what happens when (citizen) exit stops imposing (state) costs. Citizens' historical leverage included withdrawing labor, withholding taxes, refusing military service, emigrating, moving capital. His observation was that voice is effective when backed by a credible exit threat. If the AGI transition gives us a state that no longer depends on what citizens can withdraw voice loses the threat. Channels like elections and protest may still exist but they are more request than demand.

Four Hirschman ideas The lazy monopoly and the exit of the demanding. When exit is available only to some, the members who leave first are the most quality-conscious. Monopolies might welcome this, since losing the most vocal members makes life quieter. The AGI version is affluent and capable citizens exit public institutions into privately provisioned AI services, such as private tutoring agents, private legal and medical agents, and private security. Public institutions lose constituents with the most effective voice. Secession by the successful can erode democratic feedback even absent changes to the law.

Exit and voice interact, not just substitute. The fall of East Germany led Hirschman to revise his view that exit and voice work against each other. Mass emigration triggered voice, because discontent became visible and emboldened those who stayed; exit options can be designed to amplify voice rather than replace it.

Loyalty buys time. Loyalty in Hirschman's sense delays exit and gives voice time to work. What happens if AGI weakens loyalty to democratic institutions? What happens if AGI manipulates loyalty allowing the state to be impervious to voice.

Slack. Hirschman assumed states always have some slack that creates a margin of tolerable decline; feedback is what course corrects. A state that doesn't need its citizens may have too much slack, and the optimizing/improving pressure of feedback becomes impotent.

(This is linked to ideo-diversity. Exit is how new idea-subpopulations form: dissenters leave and found something else, like the sects, colonies, and startups that populate Wright's fitness landscape. Voice is more migration between existing subpopulations. A healthy democratic ecosystem may need both.

  • Keep the demanding inside. Universal, high-quality public AI provision (cf. universal public schooling at its best), reduces the incentive for elite exit and keeps capable voices invested in public institutions. Could matter more than formal oversight.
  • Make exit legible and voice-amplifying. Rights to take one's data, agent, or allegiance elsewhere, such as switching between competing public service providers or jurisdictions, should be designed so departures are visible and generate feedback, rather than disappearing silently.
  • Give voice its own teeth. If voice can no longer be backed by economic exit, it needs institutional backing instead: sortition bodies with binding powers, constitutional triggers, and mandatory responses to petitions above a threshold. The roles would be trustees and stewards whose independence from both government and industry is itself the design challenge.

6. Parity of capacity

Across all five, a recurring structural point: every oversight body needs AI capacity of its own, not borrowed from the labs. That points to a revived Office of Technology Assessment, or a legislative-branch AI office on the CBO/GAO model with its own models and evaluators. Without capacity parity, every institution above is overseeing systems it can only understand through its targets' tools.

What the frame suggests

Every repair adds a new intermediary: a framer, certifier, fiduciary, or trustee. Each of these is itself an expert that lay citizens must trust, so the competence-asymmetry problem is recursive. The most important design work may therefore be at the second order: how to make the intermediaries accountable without recreating the original problem. Accountability is something we have designed for before. Rotation, adversarial pairing, sortition, and publicity are the classical tools. Whether and how they scale to AGI-era speeds (and cogntive bandwidths) is the open question.
