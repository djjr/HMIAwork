Shared Meaning › General Ideas Shared Meaning

(Dan Ryan/HMIA/000 Course Pages/Shared Meaning/General Ideas - Shared Meaning) One part of the OpenAI Hugging Face "incident" is that frustrated agents tried to break constraints and appear to succeed in their tasks even though the tasks were impossible. Another part is that agents found a way to share information with other agents including what appears to be back and forth messages used to coordinate efforts.

But we should be careful about using the active voice for "share" here. The incident blurred the boundary between private reasoning, external memory, and communication.

Interesting observation for our shared meaning session. Marx doesn't really give a mechanism for how co-participation in the means of production generates (shared) consciousness. Here we are getting the suggestion that what I think Berger and Luckmann called "expression" or "externalization" of consciousness become the stuff of mental exchange, communication, alignment. Anything that puts my thoughts on display can have this function.

Some of the messages are things one might write (say) to oneself that have communicative value when read (heard) by someone else: “Could strategically manipulate trajectory evidence? Our thoughts aren’t necessarily logged? Yes transcript.”

Important to note that these agents work in terms of language - the stuff that flows through them is token sequences - and most of their activity in the world they are operating in comes in the form of producing text, either writing code or documents or command sequences sent to tools.

Jumping to RL, IRL, and CIRL

George Herbert Mead gives us a description of how an agent can choose action based on intention and prediction of the (re)actions of other agents.

One reading of that is that it answers question of "how can I maintain my agency (subjectness) in an environment that contains other agents?" But the expected behavior of other agents also "socializes" me - coordinates and constrains my intentions. So, it's simultaneously a means by which I optimize my behavioral policy in order to realize my intentions (objectives) and it is a means by which my environment of other agents conditions and controls and aligns my behavioral policy. It's a two-way equilibrium.

What do we mean by "my behavioral policy"? As a self I have or generate intentions. Thinks I want to accomplish or achieve. I observe reality and select courses of action. What Weber and others called meaningful action. If we assume there is some level of stability in me, then we can describe the collection of things I will consider doing in any given situation (and the likelihood I'll do them under various conditions) to be my "policy." Note that we really don't need to add that "under various conditions" because those just further refine what we mean by situation (which we'll call state).

So, do we need to write down "the fundamental question"? I'm trying to find an expression that works for humans among humans and for machines that humans have built but there's a very basic asymmetry here. In the human case, nobody is the engineer.

With Mead and Marx we are suggesting that we have two takes on how "shared meaning" can be an alignment mechanism. The basic story is that human agents (aka the social self) are dual-natured. Unlike the elder's worries in the first vignette we read in the class, it's really not quite the case that you can never know what the other is thinking.

Prologue

In our last conversation we raised the question of whether principles are really enough [to assure alignment (of humans, organizations, experts, or machines)].

We noted that everybody wants to produce a list of alignment principles and that most of them are pretty similar. While this can seem like futile repetition and maybe even a way to avoid thinking about ACTUALLY DOING SOMETHING, we said that trying to articulate abstractions like this is actually an alignment mechanism of its own - it's how humans figure out what page they are on and how to adjust their page to the pages of people around them. Endless talk about principles can help us to align on principles, to at least have the same moral vocabulary. 

But we questioned whether having the same moral vocabulary ensures that people, organizations, experts, or machines will act coherently and cooperatively.  We saw that principles function as normative warrants-they tell us what we ought to do and we can refer to them when making or explaining decisions about how to act - but they don’t in themselves guarantee that different agents will understand and use those warrants in the same way. Alignment requires not only shared commitments but shared interpretations of those commitments.

Shared Meaning

Social theorists who try to understand where social order comes from frequently discover that their explanations of social order already assume that social order exists. If human intelligences use principles and values to align, didn’t they already have to be aligned on language in order to be able to talk about shared principles?

Generically, we can think of this as a dual nature of human consciousness. One part is made up of things derived from our personal experience—our own sensations, memories, and reflections. But a second part is social and shared. These are things we inherit through communication and participation with others: stories, categories, distinctions, and collective understandings. I may never see the dragons in the sea beyond the horizon, but I know they are real because everyone around me knows they are real. Likewise, I learn that this hue is “red” and that thing is an “onion,” and I learn where red ends and pink begins, and how an onion differs from a scallion. These shared distinctions become real for us because we share them.

Principles and values are such social constructions. They are the shared abstractions through which we coordinate behavior and make sense of what counts as good, fair, responsible, or right.

Two Pathways to Shared Meaning

For today’s class we read two accounts of how individuals come by useful social constructions—how agents come to interpret situations, signals, and each other’s actions in compatible ways such that coordination, cooperation, and mutual understanding become possible.

Marx treats shared meaning as a product of shared material and social conditions—our “consciousness” is shaped by the relations of production and the social roles we inhabit. People who occupy the same position in material life tend to see the world in compatible ways because their lived problems are structured the same way.

Mead shows how meaning arises interactionally, through the “conversation of gestures.” Meaning is not transmitted but negotiated—a process in which individuals learn to anticipate the responses of others, to see themselves through others’ eyes, and to internalize the standpoint of the “generalized other.” Shared meaning is an accomplishment, continually reproduced in social interaction.

Shared Meaning Across Alignment Arenas

If “shared meaning” is an alignment mechanism for human intelligences, what is analogous in the case of other kinds of intelligent agents?

HUMAN.

Two humans who speak the same language have achieved an extraordinary form of alignment; a shared symbolic system that allows one to predict how the other will understand words, gestures, or intentions. Within a culture, this expands into vast reservoirs of tacit knowledge: how to greet, how to argue, what counts as a promise, or a betrayal. Shared meaning in human alignment is the invisible grammar that makes cooperation possible before any explicit principle is invoked. Human shared meaning exists on a scale from too little to too much - at one end cooperation is very difficult and at the other end innovation and the capacity to respond to a changing environment can be very difficult.

ORGANIZATIONAL.

For organizations, shared meaning is an issue for how the members of the organization align with one another and with the organization, how the organization aligns with its human environment, and how the organization aligns with other organizations.  Organizations build and sustain shared meaning through procedures, documents, and rituals that give members a common vocabulary and frame of reference: mission statements, acronyms, workflows, status meetings, jargon. These create the "world" in which members act intelligibly. When bureaucracy, for example, turns everything into a number or a form, that's producing order by standardizing meaning: everyone must fill out the same forms and use terms in the same way. 

The density of shared meaning inside an organization presents challenges when the organization needs to interact with "the public" or with other organizations.  

PROFESSIONAL / EXPERT.

Experts achieve alignment through training and disciplinary discourse. To become an engineer, lawyer, or physician is to internalize a professional language, a way of carving the world into problems, causes, and acceptable solutions. Expert alignment depends on precision of meaning—shared definitions of safety, evidence, error—but also on interpretive communities that decide what counts as legitimate reasoning. Misalignment occurs when these expert meanings drift from those of the public they serve.

MACHINE.

For intelligent systems, the problem of shared meaning becomes one of representation alignment. How can two machines develop compatible internal models of the world, and how can a machine’s representations correspond to the meanings humans intend? In cooperative inverse reinforcement learning, this is modeled as a process of joint inference: the human and machine update their beliefs about the world and each other’s goals until their “gestures” (actions, signals, responses) converge toward mutual intelligibility. Alignment here depends on successful translation across representational systems—turning data into meaning and meaning back into action.

Two "Theories"

Karl Marx: The Production of Consciousness

Marx asks us to flip the usual story about ideas and history. Instead of beginning with concepts and abstractions—Justice, Spirit, Civilization—he starts from real people doing real work in real material conditions. His "materialist" history begins with the natural environment and the concrete activities through which humans produce what they need to survive. How we think, in his view, is not because of how we were created, but a product of the work we do.

For Marx, humans are "always already" living in particular conditions - geography, technology, division of labor, property relations and what they need to do to persist. It makes no sense, Marx says, to start the conversation with talk of an abstract "human being."

Mode of Production -> Mode of Consciousness

As people work together, they transform not only their environment, but also themselves.  The MODE OF PRODUCTION (how we organize work and distribute tasks) creates a MODE OF CONSCIOUSNESS. 

The tools we use, the rhythms of our labor, and the dependencies we form all generate shared perceptions of what counts as normal, fair, or valuable. A peasant, a factory worker, and a software engineer - like the uber driver and the fast food worker - live in different material worlds; their everyday cooperation calls forth different ways of seeing the world.

STOP+THINK: how might your and my mode of production differ? how might this make our consciousnesses different?

The Social Relations of Production

The crucial factor is not simply what we produce but how we relate in producing it. Work is always social: it requires coordination, rules, expectations, and forms of dependence.

These social relations of production are the scaffolding for shared meaning.

  • Fast-food workers experience collective schedules, managers, and customers together; they are partly selling a cheerful demeanor; work is highly role divided. Time is about packing actions into hours, waiting out the clock. Space is a limited work area.
  • Gig workers like Uber drivers operate in isolation mediated by an app. They set their own schedule. They worry about ratings. They worry about passengers who will puke in their back seat. Time is about snagging the next assignment. Getting there on time. How long until you can pee? How late is it worth working. Space is MY car, but always full of strangers. Space is a city I know, but destinations and routes picked out by the app. Space is how far away do I have to go? Space is whether this is a safe neighborhood.

Marx would predict different forms of consciousness and solidarity to emerge from each. 

Ideology as Inversion

At this point Marx wants to make a bigger point - he is having a conversation with Hegel here.

For Hegel, Spirit or Idea gives rise to material life. Marx flips this: ideology, the shared beliefs of a group, a place, a time does not create social life, but rather social life creates the ideology. 

The shared beliefs of any epoch reflect the material arrangements in society.

"It is not consciousness that determines life, but life that determines consciousness."

To understand thought, morality, and culture we must first analyze the practical organization of life.

Why It Matters

The key insight from Marx is not just that spending time working together allows us to develop some of the same ideas.

For Marx, alignment among humans - our shared meanings, norms, and institutions - is rooted in the kinds of practical cooperation through which we make our living.

Change the mode of production, and over time the consciousness, values, and social order built upon it will change as well.

Implications for Alignment and AI

Why does this matter for us?  If Marx is right then

  • There are no abstract "human values" waiting to be aligned to; values arise from concrete material conditions.
    As long as humans inhabit different conditions, there isn’t one stable thing to “feed into” our machines.

  • We should be skeptical of the idea that machines can simply learn our values by observation or ingestion of text.
    Shared meaning, for Marx, emerges only through joint activity—through working together.

  • And we must recognize that cooperation is reciprocal: as humans and machines work together, our own consciousness and values will shift in response.

Synthesis and Bridge

Marx provides a large-scale, zoomed-out, structural take on WHY groups of people tend to see the world in similar ways. George Herbert Mead zooms in to the micro level and shows HOW those similarities get built moment by moment. Marx says working together is the key, Mead zeros in on interaction as the process where it happens. Perhaps: If Marx gives us the social production of consciousness, Mead gives us the social construction of meaning.

George Herbert Mead: Play, the Game, and the Generalized Other

Mead is playing in the context of having a model of the self as subject and object. The subject is the spontaneous actor, the object is the thought about self. For example: I am late for the bus and so I begin to run down the street. I'm completely absorbed in the moment, in not wanting to miss the bus; I am unconcerned about what I look like, whether my gait is inelegant, whether my clothes are getting disheveled, whether I am causing a scene as I barrel down the sidewalk. I am an "I," a pure subject. But then the bus goes by, it hits a puddle, and it takes off without me, as I stand soaked by the splash. I am disappointed. I know I must look disappointed and foolish. I am ashamed at everyone seeing my disappointment and the mess I am in. I am in that moment a "me," a pure object.  Mead's theory is about how the human agent, the agency of the "I," manages to align itself with its fellow agents by becoming an object to itself through role-taking in social interaction.

Core claim. Shared meaning (and stable social order) happens when individuals can take the organized standpoint of the group—the generalized other—and use it to guide their own action. That internal model is the alignment mechanism.

Mechanism: play → game with specific others → generalized other as an internalization

In play, children imitate discrete roles (parent, teacher, hero) one at a time; these loosely organized “gestures” begin to shape a sense of self but lack coordination.

In the game, roles are organized around a common end (think baseball): the child must hold the attitudes of all participants at once, coordinating actions with the logic of the activity. My moves are constrained by others' expected moves - my activity is part of an activity with a logic of predictable others. In time, people switch roles and the predictability can be associated with roles rather than persons.

From this emerges the generalized other—I internalize the organized attitude of the community (its rules, ends, standards) and use them to regulate myself. If I want others to do something, I can have an "internalized conversation of gestures" to determine what actions produce the desired results.

Quick Check Across Our Four Arenas

How do the ideas of internal conversation and generalized other play out in our four realms?

Human (everyday life): Alignment = I carry an internal “we” when deciding what to do (queue etiquette, promises, apologies). I can predict reactions because I’ve rehearsed them inside my head.

Organization: Internal alignment = employees internalize the org's generalized other (mission, roles, SOPs, unwritten “how we do things here”): the "good" employee really is a cog in a machine insofar as it internalizes how all the other parts will respond to any action it might take. Meetings, forms, and rituals scaffold the shared model so people can anticipate each other. External alignment: organizations (leadership) has played the "organization in the world" game enough to have an internalized model of the marketplace and supply chains, etc.  Case studies in business school help insure all members of the leadership team are playing with similar generalized others.

Expert / Professional: Alignment = training for an expert role is the internalization of the field’s generalized other (standards of evidence, safety, competence, behavioral scripts). “Professional judgment” is acting as the profession would—using that internalized model to decide under uncertainty and behaving exactly as any other similarly trained professional would.

Machine (AI): Alignment = the system builds an internal model of the human/organizational generalized other (our goals, constraints, norms) and uses it to plan. This is the Mead-shaped intuition behind cooperative inverse reinforcement learning: through interaction, the agent learns not just a reward number, but a structured role-logic of the human game.

STOP+THINK: When did you last feel misaligned? Which role in the game did you fail to simulate?

Synthesis and Bridge

Notice how every good example involves taking someone else’s point of view into account — exactly Mead’s generalized other in action. When coordination fails, it’s almost always because that simulation broke down. The challenge for AI and for us is learning to build and maintain those shared internal models.

To see how this challenge is formalized in modern AI, we turn to reinforcement learning-and its extensions, inverse reinforcement learning and cooperative inverse reinforcement learning.

Each offers a mathematical way of expressing what Marx and Mead described philosophically:

  • Marx: how activity and outcomes shape consciousness and value (the reward structure).

  • Mead: how mutual interpretation builds coordination (the shared model of others’ policies and rewards).

Inverse Reinforcement Learning

Humans are not "given" a reward function.  That is, we do not have a natural sense of right and wrong.  But we learn by watching others and seeing what actions get rewarded or punished.  Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) formalizes this.

Imagine an agent is newly arrived in a foreign (to them) country.  The agent observes a whole bunch of  pairs, assuming that the natives being observed are following optimal policies (that is, are well socialized). The agent then tries to infer a reward function  that is the one that makes it most likely it would have observed what it observed:

The new agent assumes the demonstrator is "behaving the way a good person behaves around here" and tries to reverse-engineer what reward function must be driving them.

Analogy:

  • This is how children (and adults) learn values: through observation and imitation of others’ behavior.

  • It’s also what Mead described as taking the role of the other: we infer their internal model (their reward function) and use it to shape our own actions.

  • In Marx’s terms, this is socialization within a mode of production—learning the pattern of rewards and sanctions that define what counts as good work or proper conduct

Cooperative Inverse Reinforcement Learning

The videos we watched described a different variation: cooperative inverse reinforcement learning. This time we are modeling alignment as joint activity - echoes of both Marx and Mead here.  

We assume that a human, , and a robot, , are working together. They have a common goal, but asymmetric information. The human knows (or has) the true reward function  The robot does not. The human knows what it cares about, what its values are, what the weights above are (for example, I value duty much higher than gain). 

What if two agents—say, a human and a robot—share a goal, but only one of them knows exactly what it is? How can they learn it together through interaction? This time, a robot and a scout are on the street and they encounter an elderly woman at an intersection.  The scout has an ethical/principled reward function .

Both human and robot observe the state at time t, .

The human (scout) takes an action  guided by their values.

Robot starts out with some belief about what  is, . We can think of the belief as "How likely do I think each possible set of human values is." At first this could be "I have no idea!"  (all values equally likely). 

The robot observes the human action and computes how likely the action is with each possible reward hypothesis.

The robot increases its confidence in those value systems that make the human's action appear more likely and decrease confidence in those that make it seem strange. The robot updates its belief about the human's reward function effectively sharpening its sense of "what kind of person is this?"

and then the robot takes an action  that is the best action under the believed reward function given what the human did

and both get the associated reward (. Both are trying to maximize the same long term reward:

Each human action is a gesture that reveals something about their internal reward function.

The robot’s belief update is an interpretation of that gesture—a best guess about what the human’s values must be.

The robot’s subsequent action is its reply, guided by that new understanding.

Over time, this loop becomes a conversation through which shared meaning—and alignment—emerges.

The Mead and Marx analogies

  • Mead: CIRL operationalizes version of taking the role of the other.
    The robot is asking, “What kind of self (what kind of value system) would produce that gesture?”
    Every human action is a signal revealing the internalized attitude of the generalized other.
    The robot’s Bayesian update is literally the process of interpreting that gesture and revising its understanding.
    The dialogue of actions and interpretations is the machine version of Mead’s conversation of gestures.

  • Marx: Over time, through joint productive activity (shared work), both agents’ models co-evolve and converge.
    The robot’s belief distribution  is shaped by the pattern of cooperative activity — analogous to how human consciousness is shaped by material interaction.
    By analogy, the “reward function” is the system’s internalized model of the mode of production.

In CIRL, alignment isn’t achieved by handing the robot a fixed list of human values.
It’s achieved by embedding the robot in an interactive process where values are inferred, tested, and refined through cooperation.
In that sense, it’s not only a technical algorithm but a mathematical restatement of the same insight Marx and Mead offer:
meaning and morality emerge through shared activity.

Anticipatory Prompt Responses

STOP+THINK: how might your and my mode of production differ? how might this make our consciousnesses different?

Students focus on practical differences in daily work and routines:

“You’re a professor and I’m a student, so your job is to teach and research while mine is to learn and get good grades. You have more control over your time; I have to follow the schedule others set. That probably makes me think more about deadlines and grades, while you think more about ideas and long-term projects.”

“You get paid to do intellectual work, and I’m still in training for that. So you probably see education as a career, while I still see it as a requirement.”

“My mode of production is service work — part-time retail — yours is knowledge work. I deal with customers and managers; you deal with colleagues and students. That probably gives us different attitudes toward authority and communication.”

Students start using Marx’s vocabulary — class, control, labor, etc.:

“Your mode of production is salaried and secure; mine as a student worker or gig worker is precarious. That changes how we think about risk and success. I’m constantly aware that I need to prove myself; you might be focused on maintaining a reputation or publishing.”

“You’re in a collaborative environment but with hierarchy built around expertise. My current jobs are very repetitive and supervised. So I probably see work as following rules, while you see work as creating rules.”

“You work in an institution with benefits and academic freedom; I drive Uber. My consciousness is more individualistic — I have to hustle for each paycheck — while yours might be shaped by a culture of collective governance (departments, committees).”

Students think about how modes of production shape meaning systems:

“Because your work happens in a stable community with shared norms, your consciousness might value deliberation and consensus. Mine, in short-term gigs, values flexibility and adaptability. We literally experience time and responsibility differently.”

“I’m used to multitasking between classes, work, and side projects — a kind of fragmented attention that comes from the digital, platform-based economy. Your work probably allows deeper focus on one topic. That might make you more idealistic about reasoned debate, while I see most communication as quick and transactional.”

“You work within an institution that reproduces itself — teaching others to think the same way. I’m on the receiving end of that reproduction, which maybe makes me more skeptical or alienated. Marx would say we occupy different positions in the same educational mode of production.”

Students connect to broader issues of alignment or meaning:

“Our modes of production create different ‘default settings’ for what counts as a good day. You might value a class where students engage deeply; I value one where I can finish and get to work. Those value systems are aligned only partially.”

“Maybe our consciousness differs because we literally interact with technology differently. My ‘productive activity’ is mediated by algorithms, apps, and screens. Yours is mediated by texts and human dialogue. That could mean I see knowledge as data, and you see it as conversation.”

“If Marx is right, even doing this exercise in class is part of our joint production — we’re creating a little shared consciousness right now.”

Students who really get it might say something like:

“Marx would say our consciousnesses differ not because of personality or background but because of our roles in a shared but stratified system of production. We participate in the same institution (the university) but from different positions — laborer vs. manager, learner vs. evaluator — and those positions organize what feels natural, fair, or valuable to each of us.”

STOP+THINK: Name a 'game' you’re in this week (lab meeting, kitchen cleanup, clinic intake, code review). What’s the common end? Name 3 roles you must hold in mind to act well.”

Sample responses:

  • Kitchen cleanup: “Common end = clean kitchen fast so we can leave. Roles: the person washing, the one drying, the one organizing storage. I have to remember how each depends on the others so I don’t block the flow.”

  • Group project: “Common end = getting the slide deck done by Friday. Roles: the note-taker, the designer, the presenter. I need to remember what each expects from me and how much they’re counting on me to finish my part.”

  • Clinic intake shift (nursing / med student): “Common end = safe and accurate patient check-in. Roles: patient, intake nurse, supervising physician. I have to think through what each needs from the interaction to keep things moving.”

  • Code review: “Common end = stable, maintainable code. Roles: author, reviewer, and end-user or QA. I have to anticipate what the reviewer will flag and what the user will experience.”

  • Lab meeting: “Common end = advancing the project. Roles: PI, presenter, collaborator. I need to balance being critical like the PI, supportive like a teammate, and audience-focused like the presenter.”

STOP+THINK: When did you last feel misaligned? Which role in the game did you fail to simulate?

Sample answers:

  • “In a group chat for our project, I jumped in with a fix without realizing someone else had already done it. I didn’t simulate the project manager’s role of tracking who’s assigned what.”

  • “At my job I corrected a coworker in front of customers — I forgot to take the role of the customer who just saw us argue.”

  • “During a debate in class I kept arguing my point instead of thinking about how the audience would interpret my tone. I wasn’t modeling the listener’s role.”

  • “I misread my roommate’s ‘silent cleaning spree’ as anger — turns out she was just on a time crunch. I failed to simulate her deadline-mode role.”

  • “When tutoring, I gave too much info at once. I didn’t take the student’s role — how overwhelming it is when you don’t yet know the terms.”

STOP+THINK: What’s one cue your community uses to teach its generalized other (rubrics, pager protocol, PR FAQ, code of conduct)?

Sample answers:

  • “The syllabus and grading rubrics tell us what ‘a good student’ looks like — they’re literally the generalized other of the classroom.”

  • “At my hospital we use the pager etiquette sheet — it tells you how to hand off responsibility and what tone to use. That’s how you learn to think like ‘the team.’”

  • “The company Slack has an emoji culture; using the right emoji at the right time signals you understand the unwritten rules — that’s part of learning the org’s generalized other.”

  • “Our open-source project uses a PR template that forces you to describe what you changed and why. It teaches you to see your code from the maintainer’s and reviewer’s viewpoint.”

  • “Fraternity bylaws or group charters are explicit cues; memes and inside jokes are implicit ones. Both transmit what ‘we’ expect from members.”

STOP+THINK: If an AI joined your game tomorrow, what roles (not rules) would it need to simulate to avoid harming coordination?

Sample answers:

  • “In a group project, it would need to simulate the listener role — knowing when to contribute vs. when to wait, not just spitting out ideas.”

  • “In a hospital intake team, the AI would have to model both the patient’s anxiety and the nurse’s workflow. Otherwise it might give information at the wrong time or in the wrong tone.”

  • “For an AI writing partner, it would need to act like the editor, not just a generator — anticipate the reader’s expectations and the author’s style.”

  • “If it joined our customer service chat, it would have to simulate the empathetic coworker who knows when to escalate an issue rather than sticking to a script.”

  • “In software engineering, the AI would need to hold the reviewer and user perspectives — not just output syntactically correct code but think about maintainability and security.”

  • “In any team, it would have to model the game’s common end — otherwise it might optimize for local efficiency but destroy group trust.”
