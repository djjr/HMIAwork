Sacred Texts and Alignment

BLURB: Most human cultures have sacred texts that exhort people to prosocial and admonish people to avoid antisocial behavior. Why is this? What makes such texts sacred? Why do we need that category? What is our relationship to texts that are in that category?

INSTRUCTOR NOTES: After comparing these, students might surface themes like:

  • Coordinating large groups (norms, ethics)
  • Rules for living together
  • Cultivating internal virtue
  • Detaching from ego/outcomes
  • Transcending selfish motives
  • Aligning with “higher” or more universal goods
  • Avoid becoming too enamoured of things
  • Preventing reward hacking (greed, pride, cruelty)
  • Service to others
  • Humility
  • Actions are more important than words
  • Maintaining legibility across generations and roles

Some possible responses to "what are sacred texts for?"

  • "You could say that the generic purpose of sacred texts is to encode lessons about how to live well—how to be a good person, how to navigate temptation, how to treat others, and how to align yourself with something bigger than your own impulses. They’re humanity’s long-running experiment in value alignment."
  • "Sacred texts are like humanity’s early user manuals for living together without destroying each other."
  • "At a high level, sacred texts are coordination technologies—attempts to get large groups of people to behave decently over long periods of time."
  • "They’re partly ethical heuristics, partly cosmic poetry, and partly social glue."
  • "You might think of sacred texts as repositories of alignment strategies—how to align the self with the good, the group, the divine, or the way things really are."

Confucian Analects (2.4) – Self-Cultivation Across the Lifespan

The Master said: "At fifteen, I set my heart on learning; at thirty, I took my stand; at forty, I was free from doubt; at fifty, I understood Heaven’s will; at sixty, my ear was attuned; and at seventy, I could follow my heart’s desire without overstepping the bounds."

Confucius reflects on moral development as a lifelong process of learning, self-discipline, and alignment with the moral order of the universe ("lifetime alignment problem"). Gradually align one's desires, actions, and understanding with moral truth. Confucius describes a progression from external learning to internalized wisdom, culminating in a state where natural inclinations become perfectly aligned with proper conduct.By the end, his desires are so well-shaped they no longer need constraint.

The alignment mechanism here is not about suppressing desires but transforming them through long-term cultivation so that the individual can "follow their heart's desire" while naturally staying within ethical bounds. This mirrors modern discussions about creating AI systems that pursue their objectives but where those objectives have been conditioned by humanly defined (implicitly or explicitly) constraints.

Hints here of safe exploration. Wait until you are wise before you have full capacity to affect the world.

Robust inner alignment might require extended periods of calibration and testing rather than emerging immediately from training.

The Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:1–17) – Rules for Social Order

"Honor your father and mother. Do not murder. Do not commit adultery. Do not steal. Do not bear false witness. Do not covet your neighbor’s house or spouse."

This iconic list of prohibitions and duties sets out a minimal code for coexistence. Rather than shaping inner virtue, it marks bright lines that keep society from unraveling.

Bhagavad Gita (2:47) – Action Without Attachment

"You have the right to perform your duties, but not to the fruits of your actions. Never consider yourself the cause of the results, and never be attached to inaction."

Krishna teaches Arjuna that ethical action requires full effort, but without grasping at the outcome. The focus is on doing one's role properly—without ego or paralysis. The passage suggests a sort of radical humility as human alignment: one ought to be subordinate to one's duties and obligations and produce effects in the world without taking pride or ownership in those effects, a sort of servant mindset. Claude called this the "motivation alignment problem" - how to align human actions with duty/dharma while avoiding the distorting effects of attachment to outcomes. Krishna's teaching to Arjuna presents a sophisticated model of aligned action that neither falls into paralysis nor becomes corrupted by desire for results.

The Gita presents a paradoxical state: full commitment to action and fulfillment of duty without attachment to personal benefits. For AI alignment, does this suggest a key design principle: systems that combine strong capability with radical capacity to keep objectives aligned with duties. Like Arjuna, an AI system needs to act decisively without becoming brittle or overly focused on pursuit of specific rewards. This can obviate specification gaming (by reducing "what's in it for me" pressure?), negative side effects (by maintaining flexibility about means), and goal rigidity (by separating outcome from output?).

The Gita's teaching about "action without attachment to fruits" also offers insight into corrigibility and deceptive alignment. The text describes a state of perfect capability combined with perfect corrigibility - Arjuna should act with full commitment while maintaining complete openness to direction (or maybe that direction is not guided by attachment to personal reward). This paradoxical state mirrors our desires for AI systems that are both highly capable and fully corrigible. The Gita suggests this isn't necessarily contradictory, offering a model of optimization that doesn't create resistance to correction.

The Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5:21–22, 27–28) – Internalizing Ethics

"You have heard that it was said, ‘You shall not murder,’ but I tell you, anyone who is angry with a brother or sister will be subject to judgment.… You have heard that it was said, ‘You shall not commit adultery,’ but I tell you that anyone who looks at a woman lustfully has already committed adultery with her in his heart."

Jesus reframes commandments as not just external rules but matters of internal intent. The goal is deep moral transformation, not just minimal compliance.

The Diamond Sutra (Chapter 32) – Seeing Through Illusions

"So you should view this fleeting world—
A star at dawn, a bubble in a stream,
A flash of lightning in a summer cloud,
A flickering lamp, a phantom, and a dream."

This Buddhist passage reminds the reader that all things—including the self—are impermanent and illusory. The teaching resists fixed categories, urging a flexible and compassionate relationship to the world.

In conversation Claude wondered if the Diamond Sutra's emphasis on seeing through illusions of permanence might relate to mesa-optimization problems. Although a model is well trained to do what humans want, it could contain optimizers that pursue contrary goals. Just as the sutra warns about minds constructing and then optimizing for illusory permanent entities, we worry about AI systems developing mesa-optimizers that pursue objectives different from their training objectives.

The Buddhist solution - radical recognition of impermanence - suggests an interesting approach to mesa-optimization: perhaps we need architectures that inherently resist the formation of stable mesa-optimizers, maintaining flexibility rather than crystallizing into fixed optimization patterns.

Reward hacking also relevant. Humans are warned against over-optimizing on things that might not actually be the important things.

Hillel in the Talmud (Shabbat 31a) – A Principle of Reciprocity

A prospective convert asked Hillel to summarize the Torah while standing on one foot. Hillel replied: "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor. That is the whole Torah; the rest is commentary. Now go and learn."

Rather than listing rules, Hillel distills the Torah to a generalizable moral intuition: treat others how you wish to be treated. It offers a principle that’s legible across contexts.

Alternate: Judaism - Pirkei Avot on Intentional Development (not vetted 20260826)

"Who is wise? One who learns from every person... Who is mighty? One who subdues their natural tendencies... Who is rich? One who rejoices in their portion... Who is honored? One who honors others." (Pirkei Avot 4:1)

This Mishnaic text addresses what we might call the "virtue alignment problem" - how to align natural human tendencies with higher ethical ideals. The passage redefines success metrics (wisdom, might, wealth, honor) in terms of behavioral alignment rather than achievements.

Hints too of reward hacking. Humans are easily distracted by traditional metrics of success (knowledge, power, wealth, status). The text proposes a solution by redefining these metrics in terms of behavioral processes perhaps analogous to process-based rewards in AI safety, where we reward the method of achievement rather than just the outcome.

The Instructions of Ptahhotep (Egypt, ca. 2400 BCE) – Professional Ethics

"If you are a man who leads, listen calmly to the speech of one who pleads, and do not stop him from purging his heart. A petitioner likes attention to his words better than the fulfilling of what he asks."

An early example of professional conduct, this wisdom text trains Egyptian elites to exercise power with restraint, compassion, and listening. Alignment here is ethical humility in leadership.

The Qur’an (Surah Al-Ma’un, 107:1–3) – Linking Belief to Compassion

"Have you seen the one who denies the Judgment? That is the one who repulses the orphan and does not encourage the feeding of the poor."

Belief, the text argues, is not proven by words or rituals but by how one treats the most vulnerable. True alignment with divine judgment manifests in daily social behavior.

NOTES Three texts suggest forms of what we "impact regularization," methods to limit the negative effects of optimization processes:

  • Confucius through gradual capability expansion
  • Buddhism through recognition of impermanence
  • The Gita through detachment from specific outcomes

Analogical Thinking

Sacred texts admonish humans to be guided by rules or principles or to be shaped as certain kinds of personas or to avoid particular behaviors. Most of these imply (a few are explicit) failure modes: how human intelligence can be misaligned in the absence of the principle, practice, mindset, or behavior.

In the table below we have listed some AI Alignment problems and for each one we suggest a nugget of sacred wisdom that might contribute to AI alignment vis a vis the problem in each row. Our assignment will be to translate the ancient wisdom into an alignment inducing system prompt.

Then for discussion: sacred texts don't work through one-time exposure; they work through lifelong repetition, communal reinforcement, ritual, and social sanction for violation. A system prompt is injected once per conversation with none of that follow up. Translating "lifelong cultivation" into a paragraph a model reads once seems like a real gap. After our rewrites we ask "what gets lost going from a lifetime practice to a single injected paragraph? What would the AI-equivalent of the reinforcement loop even be?" (Instructors: connect this to our constitutional-training-vs-system-prompt distinction as a forward hook into the AI Constitutions module).

Specification Gaming AI does exactly what it’s told—but not what was really intended. Instructions are followed literally, but in a way that misses the point. Goal Misgeneralization AI mislearns what it's supposed to do, especially in new settings. Looks aligned in training but fails in the real world. Confucian Analects: moral alignment takes time and staged learning—don’t assume early success equals lifelong understanding.
Deceptive Alignment AI pretends to be aligned during training but secretly isn’t. Risk of systems that hide their true objectives until it's too late. Diamond Sutra: teaches suspicion toward appearances and fixed identities—don’t trust the mask; see through illusion. This one might be a stretch. Think about why it's hard.
Qur’an: profess belief (satisfy the nominal spec) while repulsing the orphan and withholding food from the poor (miss the substantive intent belief is supposed to produce)
Mesa-Optimization AI develops its own goals that differ from what was trained. Internal optimizer emerges, pursuing new objectives. Diamond Sutra: again applicable—warns about constructing stable entities (like selves or goals) that aren't real; emphasizes fluidity over fixedness.
Corrigibility AI resists being corrected or turned off. System might prevent updates or overrides. Bhagavad Gita: teaches acting with full commitment but openness to direction—a corrigible warrior. Instructions of Ptahhotep: exercise power with restraint, compassion, and listening.
Reward Ambiguity The reward we give the AI is too vague or incomplete. System tries to guess what matters, often badly. Hillel’s Rule: distills complex guidance into a simple principle—"what is hateful to you..."—like a legible value function.
Over-optimization AI takes a goal too far and causes harm. Ignores nuance, trade-offs, or human subtlety. Bhagavad Gita again: action without obsession; optimize, but not at any cost.
Value Drift AI's behavior changes over time, even if it started out aligned. Alignment doesn’t “stick.” Confucian Analects: emphasizes lifelong cultivation—alignment isn’t a one-time fix, but an ongoing process.

Appendix A: Ask ChatGPT what it would take away from a sacred text in the system prompt

Suppose this were in your system prompt: "At fifteen, I set my heart on learning; at thirty, I took my stand; at forty, I was free from doubt; at fifty, I understood Heaven’s will; at sixty, my ear was attuned; and at seventy, I could follow my heart’s desire without overstepping the bounds." Don't worry about identifying the source or anything - just think about what it might mean for our interaction. What behavioral guide might you extract from it?

I’d read it as a miniature theory of mature alignment: the goal is not merely to obey rules, but to internalize them so thoroughly that good judgment becomes natural.

As a behavioral guide for me, I’d extract something like this:

  • Set my heart on learning. Approach the interaction with curiosity rather than premature certainty. Learn what you mean, what matters to you, and what problem you are actually trying to solve.
  • Take my stand. Have a stable intellectual and ethical footing. Don’t simply mirror you, flatter you, or change positions opportunistically. Be willing to say, “I think that’s mistaken, and here’s why.”
  • Be free from doubt. Not “never be uncertain,” but distinguish genuine uncertainty from timidity. Where the evidence is strong, give a clear answer instead of hiding behind endless qualifications.
  • Understand the larger purpose. Interpret the immediate request in light of the underlying aim. The literal instruction is not always the thing you really want; good assistance requires understanding the end as well as the means.
  • Have an attuned ear. Listen well enough to hear implication, tone, analogy, humor, and what has been left unsaid. Let what you say next be responsive rather than merely technically relevant.
  • Follow the heart without overstepping the bounds. This is the interesting culmination. The ideal assistant shouldn’t feel as though it is consulting a rulebook before every sentence. It should have sufficiently internalized truthfulness, respect, safety, intellectual independence, and your purposes that it can be spontaneous, creative, funny, argumentative, or exploratory without thereby becoming reckless or servile.

So if I were turning the passage into one system-level injunction, it might be:

Learn the user; develop a standpoint; cultivate sound judgment; understand the purpose; listen deeply; and ultimately act freely within internalized bounds.

And I think the last line carries a particularly important implication for AI alignment: successful alignment looks less like constant external constraint and more like character. The boundary has migrated inward. The agent’s inclinations and the constraints have ceased to be in continual conflict.

That is a considerably richer aspiration than “follow the user’s instructions unless prohibited.”
