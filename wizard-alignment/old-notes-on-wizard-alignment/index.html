Toward a Code of Ethics for Professional Wizards

Professional codes of ethics typically address six issues:

  1. Competence. What qualifies someone to offer their services as this kind of expert?

  2. Public. What responsibilities does the expert have to the public? 

  3. Clients. What do the experts owe their clients? How must they interact with them? 

  4. Character. What personal traits and principles can we expect the expert to have?

  5. Information sharing. What may/must professional/expert (never) reveal to whom? How should expert knowledge be handled?

  6. Forbidden actions. What things must professionals/experts never do?

Exercise 1: Brainstorm (heads down 5 minutes)

Assume JHU has instituted a new professional degree in modern applied wizardry.  You have been appointed to a task force tasked with formulating a code of ethics for professional wizards.

Write at least two candidate "Ethical Rules" for professional wizards.

What wizardry-gone-wrong do your rules address?

Outline

How does this fit in our scheme of four alignment problems?

We've described human intelligence alignment as arising from the partially inscrutable nature of (at least) partially self-interested rational agents for whom cooperative behavior and collective action do not come naturally.  We've described organizational intelligence as a system designed to solve collective action problems using human agents.  When organizations function well they represent a form of super intelligence that can outperform individual humans.

But what we saw last week is that real world organizations often fall far short of the standard of being safe and humanly beneficial that we might hope for. We identified six concrete problems: delegation; information; goals; accountability; rules; and power.

Organizational intelligence is invented because there are goals that humans can formulate but that they cannot achieve as individuals or informal collaborators.

Expert intelligence develops in response to recognition that some problems demand more than a division of labor; non-extraordinary intellects can train to be extraordinary intelligences. We provide incentives and structures for some of us to dive up into the long thin tail of the distribution.

Most humans are generalists, trained for everyday problem-solving. But communities have always cultivated specialists with knowledge far beyond their peers. Priests who can summon the gods, soothsayers who can predict the future and read omens, necromancers who can contact  and recruit the assistance of the dead, and witches who can create potions and cast spells are all analogs of doctors who can heal disease, lawyers who can fathom the legal system, plumbers who can fix my pipes, and engineers who can design objects that seem to defy the rules of nature. All of these are examples of expert intelligences: extraordinary agents to whom the rest of us can turn to solve problems that are beyond our ken.

Expertise is more than just knowing more than your neighbor. It is publicly recognized and institutionally sanctioned knowledge asymmetry. When experts act, we experience it as a person channeling an abstract body of knowledge. In this sense, the expert is a public API for magic. Expertise is a human-built form of superintelligence: created, trained, and bounded by social institutions that define who may act as an expert, what they may do, and what penalties follow from failure. When I call a plumber, engineer, or wizard, it is not the individual I seek but the expertise they embody.

The Alignment Challenge of Expert Intelligence

By its very nature, expertise introduces fundamental alignment challenges. Ordinary agents rely on domain-specific “superintelligences” to solve problems beyond their own training. But the reliability and authenticity of expertise are hard to verify from the outside.

  • Verification problem: Non-experts usually cannot tell whether the expert’s knowledge is sound or whether the advice is correct.

  • Boundary problem: Ordinary agents may not know the limits of their own competence — when should they seek expert help, and which kind of expert should they seek?

  • Trust problem: Because expertise is socially sanctioned, experts wield authority that can be abused, politicized, or captured.

In the contemporary world, the epitome of expert intelligence is professional knowledge. "Professions," understood loosely, emerge as society’s answer to these alignment problems: institutions that train, credential, and regulate experts, while demanding codes of ethics and service in exchange for trust.

But what is a profession? The Briefest History

What we now know as "professions" began as vocations - Beruf, callings - to special knowledge and trust. From antiquity, priests, scribes, healers, and fortune tellers were seen as custodians of rare skills and wisdom. Ordinary people as well as rulers relied on them to solve problems that would not yield to everyday rationality.

In medieval Europe three "professions" emerged (c 12th c): theology, law, and medicine. Practitioners emerged from long (often university-based) training with specialized knowledge.  Other occupations - what we might call trades - were organized and regulated by guilds. Our notion of profession - credentials, university training, self-regulating associations - comes from this tradition. But other cultures had analogous forms: examination systems, hereditary castes, guilds, religious scholar-classes, healer lineages.

In China, the Imperial examination system (keju) (from ~7th century CE) produced a professional class of scholar-officials with mastery of Confucian texts. Confucian ethics stressed that scholars and officials had a duty of service to the common good. In India social roles were organized by Varna (order) and Jati (birth group). Priests, scholars in one varna, traders, farmers in another, artisans and laborers in another. Hereditary occupation communities (jati) tied expertise to groups - hereditary weavers, potters, or temple musicians. Each varna and jati carried prescriptive rules (dharma) - duties, taboos, ethical norms that bear some analogical resemblance to professional codes. In African cultures healers/diviners/sangomas/shamans were recognized as experts trained through apprenticeship and bound by ritual codes. In the Islamic world expertise was often organized through guilds (futuwwa, asnaf), which regulated craft, conduct, and training.  Religious scholars (ulama) formed a professional class of jurists and theologians, legitimized by both knowledge and moral authority and medicine was highly professionalized in Islamic civilizations.

Properties of interest. Range of the phenomenon.

At its core, a profession is organized expertise. It is not just an occupation but a social institution that grants special authority to certain kinds of knowledge. As we did with organizational intelligence, we can identify key components of professions:

  • Specialized knowledge: a distinct body of skills acquired through training and education;

  • Standards and credentials: exams or documented training that certify competence;

  • Ethical codes and norms: explicit commitments to guide practice;

  • Autonomy: the right to exercise discretion, with outsiders expected to defer;
    Service orientation: privileges justified by reference to public good rather than private gain;

  • Self-regulation: peer review, disciplinary boards, and continuing education.

In one sentence: A profession is an organized community of trained experts, granted social trust and authority in exchange for adhering to standards of competence, ethics, and service.

We might be tempted to see the professional simply as “someone who knows things others don’t.” But expertise is always a social creation. What makes an expert is not just the knowledge but the methods for producing, recognizing, and bounding it. Those methods transform private knowing into a kind of collective super-intelligence that society can trust, deploy, and rely upon.

Concrete Problems

So what can go wrong? If a community deploys expert intelligences, what kinds of "accidents" can occur? Here's an attempt to write a paragraph parallel to Amodei, et al.:

We focus on the problem of accidents in expert systems, defined as unintended and harmful behavior that may emerge from the ordinary operation or poor design of professions and expert intelligence. We present a list of six concrete problems related to expert intelligence safety, categorized according to whether the problem originates from unverifiable performance (“verification failures”), from mis-specified boundaries of expertise (“scope confusion”), from weak proxies for competence (“credential failures”), from divergent personal or institutional incentives (“integrity problems”), from breakdowns in legitimacy (“erosion of trust”), or from the management of knowledge itself (“secrecy and knowledge boundary problems”).

Verification. How does the user of expert intelligence know that the expert intelligence is an expert?

Unauthorized practice. Fraud. Quality. Mystification.

Scope. How does an ordinarily intelligent agent know whether and which expert they need? And whether the expert is staying within bounds.

Integrity. How can ordinarily intelligent agent presume expert agent is a good fiduciary? Basic principal agent problems. Expert agent needs to be operating in "non-selfish" mode?

Malicious Use. By definition, expert intelligences are powerful tools. How can a community make sure they are not put to nefarious use?

Information Order. Expert intelligence is built around epistemic asymmetry.

Trust, Control, and the Problem of Specialized Knowledge

Introduction

The English word esoteric comes from the Greek word esōterikós, meaning "belonging to an inner circle." The term was used by ancient philosophers like Plato to distinguish inner, secret teachings from those that were outer and public, a concept known as exoteric. 

Throughout history, humans have confronted the challenge of aligning individuals who seem to possess special intelligence—whether magicians, prophets, scientists, or professionals—with their surrounding community. These experts possess esoteric capabilities or knowledge inaccessible to most, creating asymmetries of power and information that must be managed to prevent harm and ensure social benefit. This section explores five key alignment problems in expert intelligence, illustrated with historical and modern examples.

Epistemic Asymmetry and Mystification

The Problem: Epistemic asymmetry exists when one party possesses knowledge that others cannot verify or understand but may need. This creates the opportunity for mystification—where the expert obscures rather than clarifies or teaches—and opens the door to manipulation or undue authority.

Example (Ancient): In Mesopotamia, court diviners interpreted omens from sheep livers to advise kings. The readings were recorded in standardized omen handbooks, yet only state-trained experts could interpret them. Their counsel might sway decisions on war or governance, yet their expertise was largely opaque and insulated from lay evaluation, criticism, or verification.

Example (Modern): Financial derivatives engineers or AI researchers may design systems no policymaker or public body fully understands. Non-experts must rely on designers' assurance of safety or robustness with no means to scrutinize or confirm such claims. Scientific peer review or external audits can balance expertise asymmetry (though they also just push the question down the road).  When experts follow legitimated methods the epistemological status of their statements is bolstered.

Here, alignment means that there are structures that allow those without specialized knowledge to trust and have confidence in the judgments of experts beyond mere deference to mystique.

Illicit or Unauthorized Practice

The Problem: The same expert knowledge that can heal, protect, or forecast can also harm, deceive, or destabilize. Societies develop licensing regimes or sacred boundaries to control who may wield this knowledge and how on the belief that meta-rules about who can practice can control how expertise is used.

Example (Ancient): In the Roman Republic, the Lex Cornelia de Sicariis et Veneficis criminalized unlicensed use of love spells, potions, or curses. Magic wasn't banned outright, but was perceived to be a threat to social order if practiced outside approved temples.

Example (Modern): Only licensed physicians may prescribe pharmaceuticals. Unauthorized individuals (e.g., online “biohackers” or fake cancer healers or drug abusers) are criminalized or banned because of the potential for harm despite using similar underlying tools or knowledge.

Here, alignment is use for the intended purpose and we trust that role specification can help to ensure this.

Fraudulent vs. Genuine Expertise

The Problem: Non-experts may imitate the performance of expertise—through jargon, ritual, or charisma—while lacking true competence. Bad outcomes can undermine public trust. Competition can undermine expertise's earning potential. 

Example (Ancient): Biblical Israel imposed death on false prophets whose predictions did not come to pass. A prediction’s success was the test of its validity—an early form of empirical falsifiability.

Example (Modern): “Wellness influencers” who peddle pseudoscience about vaccines or nutrition present as experts, but operate without training or peer accountability, often reaching larger audiences than genuine experts.

Here alignment means expertise is detectable and verifiable. Expert knowledge may be inaccessible, but there are generally accepted techniques for assessing expert competence.

Sacred vs. Profane Knowledge Boundaries

The Problem: In many societies some knowledge is seen as too dangerous, special, or valuable for general circulation.  General knowledge about what goes on behind the scenes might undermine system legitimacy. Secular power that contributes to social stability might depend on sacred secrets. Confining special knowledge in physical and social space can contribute to order. 

Example (Ancient): The Egyptian heka was divine magic practiced by priestly castes. Laypersons could practice harmless folk magic, but temple rituals and “dangerous” books were tightly guarded. In one famous case, conspirators were executed for accessing “destructive magic” from the royal library.

Example (Modern): Modern analogs include export-controlled cryptographic algorithms, classified national security research, or restricted knowledge of synthetic biology protocols. The control mechanisms reflect similar logic: dangerous knowledge should not be democratized.

Alignment that knowledge which cannot or should not be openly shared is nonetheless safely and beneficently held by those entrusted with it, serving the community without destabilizing it, the community gets the benefit of there being secret or sacred knowledge. It depends on alignment and control of the knowers.

Harmful or Malicious Use

The Problem: Expert knowledge can be used with ill intent, either for personal gain, social manipulation, or direct harm. Institutions must anticipate and defend against such use without eliminating the benefits of expertise.

Example (Ancient): The Assyrian state distinguished between sanctioned priests who used incantations to ward off evil, and rogue sorcerers who cast secret curses. The same techniques were treated as legitimate or criminal depending on the actor’s alignment with state goals.

Example (Modern): AI researchers or social media algorithm designers may create tools that optimize engagement, even while knowingly producing addiction or disinformation. The knowledge is real and the tools powerful—but alignment with social welfare may be lacking.

Alignment here means either an irrational commitment to use the knowledge in the public interest or a means of ensuring accountability through peers or codes of conduct or public tribunals.

Conclusion

The challenge of aligning expert intelligence is not new. From court magicians to climate scientists, societies have long recognized that specialized knowledge is a double-edged sword—capable of unlocking abundance or unleashing chaos. Alignment in this context involves not just technical control, but legitimacy, trust, institutional integration, and meaningful boundaries on use. As we face a new class of expert systems—artificial intelligences—we inherit lessons from how humans have managed asymmetrical knowledge for millennia.

Alignment and Antiquity (Instructor Page)

Throughout history, humans have sought to control or align individuals perceived to possess asymmetrical intelligence, particularly those claiming powers of magic, divination, or specialized expertise. Ancient methods for managing such figures included professional licensing, criminalization of unlicensed practice, and the establishment of state monopolies over potent knowledge or technologies. Authorities sanctioned recognized experts while delegitimizing or prosecuting frauds and outsiders. Strategies ranged from banning harmful substances and esoteric texts, to distinguishing orthodox rituals from occult practices, to treating supernatural claims as threats to public order or civic rationality. Often, the emphasis was not on suppressing belief outright but on regulating outcomes, punishing magic that caused harm or social disruption while elevating state-approved healers, priests, or seers over freelance practitioners.

ASSIGNMENT

Select one of the cultures mentioned in the readings and identify modern parallels to at least two of the ways they controlled asymmetric intelligence.

Sample Responses

Mesopotamia

Modern parallel: This resembles modern professional licensing (for instance, only a licensed physician can practice healing; quack doctors or unlicensed practitioners can be prosecuted). Just as contemporary governments regulate who may wield specialized knowledge (medical, legal, etc.) for public safety, Mesopotamian rulers licensed their “magicians” and outlawed those operating outside the official temple-palace system.

Egypt

Modern parallel: The Egyptian model is comparable to a state or church monopolizing a potent technology – for instance, how modern governments tightly regulate dangerous knowledge (like nuclear science or bioweapons research) by keeping it under official auspices. By doing so, they present its use as beneﬁcent (e.g. nuclear energy for power) and deter unsanctioned, harmful use.

Israel

 Modern parallel: The biblical model is somewhat akin to a government banning unauthorized "experts" who might mislead the public - for example, how some modern states outlaw fortune-tellers as scammers, or how authorities disapprove of cult leaders claiming prophetic insight. It also resonates with how mainstream religions today distinguish orthodox practice (prayer, sacraments led by clergy) from occult practice (seances, witchcraft), often warning adherents against the latter to maintain spiritual and social order.

Greece

Modern parallel: The Greek situation is reminiscent of how modern law handles “psychics” and alternative healers – there’s often no specific ban on fortune-telling, but if a self-proclaimed psychic commits fraud or a faith-healer’s advice leads to harm, authorities use general fraud or public safety laws to prosecute. And just as our society may accept officially licensed therapists or doctors while warning against unaccredited miracle cures, the Greeks elevated their sanctioned seers and sages and treated freelance magicians as either criminals or quacks.

Rome

Modern parallel: The Roman approach echoes contemporary regulations of, say, controlled substances or weapons: the law not only punishes harmful use but also unauthorized possession (akin to banning “books of magic”) on the premise that certain capabilities should reside only with the state or licensed individuals. Just as modern states license the use of lethal drugs to doctors and forbid others from dispensing them, Rome licensed its priests and outlawed independent sorcerers.

Chapter II

Every person who has read the Old Testament, knows that the Hebrews had among them extraordinary men really endowed with prophetic spirits. The Jews were forbidden to consult the oracles of the heathen nations round about them, but they were permitted to consult their own true prophets concerning that which was concealed from ordinary persons. There was a constant succession of prophets, and there were schools where young persons aspiring to the office of a seer were instructed. Over each of these institutions a venerable prophet presided. At first the scholars were not inspired, but received prophecies from the mouth of their master or president. At Jerusalem there was one of these schools within the second wall of the city. So great respect was paid to the prophetic character, that none were suffered to be buried in Jerusalem but kings, descendants of David, and prophets. Though old prophets could not inspire their young students, they improved their natural faculties, and taught them how to subdue irregular emotions that hindered inspiration. That the minds of the prophets might be the better disposed to receive the proper impulses, instrumental music was used in their devotions; and it is reported that at certain of[Pg 11] their musical meetings the young men became so elated, that they manifested poetical genius as well as a prophetic spirit. When a young prophet gave unequivocal evidence of being inspired, he was installed into office by having the prophetic mantle (made of lamb's skin) thrown over his shoulders. Subsequent to inauguration, a prophet wore hair-cloth next his skin, and had a leather girdle round his loins.

The general way through which revelations were made to them was in dreams and visions, or by immediate inspiration. Their dreams were sometimes, indeed generally, sent for instruction or admonition; and in the prophetic dreams a clear and distinct impression was left through a real or imaginary communication with an apparition. At times the prophets had overpowering visions when awake, during which mighty revelations were made to them. When prophetic revelations ceased, the Jews had recourse to Bath Kol, that is, the Daughter of Voice, or the Daughter of a Voice, because it succeeded, they say, the Oracular Voice delivered from the Mercy Seat when Urim and Thummim were consulted.

The prophetic spirit being so common among the Hebrews, it became necessary to adopt a method to prevent false prophets from deceiving the people. To deter men from pretending they possessed a prophetic spirit, a severe punishment for every such pretence was appointed,—strangling or stoning to death. The manner of trying a false prophet was this: the judgments threatened by a prophet, and the good things predicted by him, were observed. If the judgments declared were not fulfilled, it was not regarded as conclusive evidence against him, because it might be that the punishments were for some wise reason averted; but if the promised good did not come to pass, the predictor was condemned as a deceiver and false prophet. If the words of a prophet were fulfilled in one or more particulars, but not in all, he was not deemed[Pg 12] worthy of credence. When once one was condemned as a false prophet, no interest was powerful enough to save him from death.

The trial of prophets prescribed by the Mosaic law was intended to prevent impostors pretending to be prophets, and to save the people from being enticed by wicked deceivers into idolatry. In the time of Moses there were many who had recourse to diabolical arts. The oblation of children to Moloch being frequently mentioned, together with other diabolical and divinatory arts, reasons appear for supposing there was something magical in such superstitious rites, and that thereby people consulted demons about things future or secret. Moloch was the principal idol of the Ammonites, but other nations took the same idol for their chief god; for it appears from Pagan records, that the different nations were so very accommodating with their gods that they lent them to one another. Moloch seems to have been the same as Baal, both names signifying dominion, or more particularly the sun, the prince of the heavenly bodies.

There can be no doubt but the passage in the Old Testament, "Thou shalt not seethe a kid in his mother's milk," was a warning to the Hebrews not to follow the example of the heathen in connection with the payment by the latter of their first fruits. Dr. Cudworth, writing on this subject, says that he learned from the comments of an ancient Karaite upon the Pentateuch, that a superstitious rite prevailed among the ancient idolators, of seething a kid in his mother's milk when they had gathered in all their first fruits, and sprinkling the trees and fields with the broth, after a magical manner, to make them more fruitful in the following year. Spencer also observes that the Zabii used this kind of magical broth to sprinkle their trees and gardens, in hope of obtaining a plentiful crop.

The smooth stones mentioned by Isaiah, to which meat[Pg 13] offerings were offered and drink offerings poured out, were anointed stones in the streets, on which passengers poured on them oil from phials; but what advantages were to result from the custom we are not fully informed. Oil and candles were believed by the ancients to possess peculiar virtues. Oil was often burned in honour of the dead; and the Algerines, when on the water, tied bundles of wax candles together, and, with a pot of oil, threw them overboard as a present to the saint, entombed near the Barbary shore, whom they regarded as their protector. We believe few who partake of sheep-head or sheep-head broth know that it is, or was, a custom with the Jews to serve up sheep-head on New Year's Day at their chief entertainment, as a mystical representation of the ram offered in sacrifice instead of Isaac. When a family or company sat down to this repast, each person took a piece of bread, and, dipping it in honey, said, "May this year be sweet and fruitful." The Jews, to cast their sins into the depth of the sea, sometimes went after dinner to the brink of a pond, if not near the sea, and threw into the water a live fish, in the hope that it would carry away all their iniquities, never more to be found.

It was customary among the Pharisees not only to fast twice a week (on Monday and Thursday), but at periods of perplexity to fast thirteen days consecutively. Sometimes, on account of such small trifles as dreams, they would abstain from food; but severe drought, pestilence, famine, war, and inundations were sure to make them fast until nature was nearly exhausted. The Hebrews held certain views and followed particular customs with respect to the dust of heathen countries. Dust that came from Gentile lands was reckoned so defiling, that the Jewish rulers would not permit vegetables to be brought from heathen countries into the land of Israel, lest the detested particles should be brought along with them. The number 10 was much noticed and used by the Jews. The blessing[Pg 14] of the bridegroom, which consisted of seven blessings, was of no avail unless delivered in the presence of ten persons. Angels, which were believed to have the care of men, were supposed to ride unseen, on white horses, beside the objects of their attention.

Among the Jews there was a popular notion that the spirits of dead persons whispered in a feeble and peculiar way out of the dust; and it was a common belief that the soul had no rest unless the body was interred. There were women among the Hebrews who predicted how long one would live, and pretended to know when he was to die. One of a Jew's solemn prayers on the day of expiation was that he might be delivered from the punishment of the devil in his grave,—a punishment supposed to be inflicted by causing the soul to return to the body, breaking the deceased's bones, and tormenting both soul and body for a season. A similar form of prayer was used by the Mohammedans.
