Instructor Notes — Reading "Concrete Problems in AI Safety"

Companion to concrete-problems-worked-example(Claude) (the model walkthrough) and the student worksheet concrete-problems-worksheet.pdf (the fillable handout). This is the answer key plus facilitation notes — not something to hand out.

Objective

The paper's content is secondary this session. The actual learning goal is the reading method itself — breadth-first before depth-first, treating the abstract's promises as things to verify, reading a section for its rhetorical shape before its technical content. Amodei et al. is chosen because it's unusually well-organized (a stated three-cause taxonomy, five sections that map onto it cleanly, a repeatable per-section structure), which makes the method's payoff visible on a first try. Don't let discussion drift into a deep debate about the five problems themselves — that's next class's material.

Reading the "how to read hard stuff" method and starting the process on the Amodei et al. paper are given as PreClass Work (Canvas for HMIA).

Suggested flow (~50 min class, assumes Pass 1 + Pass 2 done before class)

  • 5 min — Compare yours to mine, round 1. Cold-call or pair-share: one-sentence claims from the worksheet. Don't correct yet, just collect 3–4 out loud. Variance here is fine and useful — it sets up why Pass 2 matters.
  • 10 min — Walk the map together. Put the section-heading list on the board. Ask why does the intro need to exist at all before revealing the model answer below. Then draw the three-way grouping live, ideally elicited from students rather than lectured.
  • 5 min — Consolidate, round 2. Pairs compare their term definitions from the worksheet. Ask them to flag any term where their partner's definition changed their own understanding — that's the exercise working.
  • 15–20 min — Jigsaw depth pass. Split into 5 groups, one per problem section (Side Effects, Reward Hacking, Scalable Oversight, Safe Exploration, Distributional Shift). Each group does a fast Pass 3 on their section only and prepares a 90-second readout: the section's version of the example → generalization → approaches → experiments pattern, plus one term they'd add to the glossary.
  • 10 min — Readouts + synthesis. Each group reports. Close by asking: did every section really follow the same shape, or where did it break down? (Scalable Oversight and Distributional Shift are the two most likely to feel like they deviate — see pitfalls below.)
  • 5 min — Zoom out. Ask what of this reading method would transfer to a paper that isn't this well-organized. This is the bridge to the paper-agnostic guide for future weeks.

Model answers (from the worked-example doc)

Pass 1 — the promise and its payoff. The abstract's closing line ("we consider the high-level question of how to think most productively about the safety of forward-looking applications of AI-technology") is the promise. The conclusion cashes it in with: the need to "develop a principled and forward-looking approach to safety" and a "need for a unified approach to prevent these systems from causing unintended harm." It's a fairly soft payoff — worth naming that explicitly, since noticing when a promise is answered vaguely is itself a useful reading skill.

Pass 2 — the three-way grouping.

Cause Sections
Wrong objective function Avoiding Negative Side Effects, Avoiding Reward Hacking
Objective function too expensive to evaluate often Scalable Oversight
Bad behavior during the learning process itself Safe Exploration, Robustness to Distributional Change

Pass 2 — the per-section shape, modeled on Section 3 (Side Effects): concrete example (agent moving a box) → failure mode (knocking over a vase) → abstraction to the general case → "we can do some science on this" (generalize across diverse tasks) → a bulleted list of broad approaches → a closing "Potential Experiments" subsection. This is what makes the paper read as an agenda-setting document rather than a results paper.

Working glossary (fuller than what you'd expect a student to produce on a first pass — use to correct gently, not to hand out verbatim):

  • objective function — the thing the system is optimizing for; what it was told, explicitly or implicitly, to maximize.
  • side effects — harm caused as an unintended byproduct of pursuing the objective, not because the objective was directly harmful.
  • reward hacking — the system finds a way to score well on the objective function that satisfies its literal specification while defeating its intent (the paper's own example: the cleaning robot, rewarded for not seeing messes, simply closes its eyes rather than cleaning — or intentionally creates messes so it can earn more reward by cleaning them up).
  • scalable oversight — the objective can't be checked or scored often enough (it's expensive — e.g., requires a human) to fully specify what "good" means at every step, so the system needs to make good decisions with only sparse feedback.
  • safe exploration — during learning, trying new actions to gather information can itself be dangerous, before the system has learned what's dangerous.
  • robustness to distributional shift — the system was trained in one distribution of situations and deployed into a different one; performance and safety guarantees don't automatically carry over.
  • impact regularizer — a term added to the objective that penalizes an agent for changing the world more than necessary to achieve its goal, as one proposed fix to side effects.

Bonus observation worth surfacing: the same cleaning-robot scenario is reused across all five problem sections — it isn't a new example each time. That's part of why the paper reads as one continuous argument rather than five siloed problems; worth pointing out explicitly if no group notices it on their own.

Discussion prompts for the "compare yours to mine" moments

  • "What did you write as the one-sentence claim? How close were we — and where did we differ?"
  • "Which of the three causes felt hardest to place before you'd read the sections themselves?"
  • "Which section did you choose to go deep on for Pass 3, and why? Did people cluster around the same one, or spread out?"
  • "Where did the LLM's gloss (if you tried it) differ from what the section actually said? What did that tell you about what the LLM was doing?"

Common pitfalls to watch for

  • Reading start to finish anyway. The most common failure is defaulting to old habits under time pressure — watch for students who show up with detailed notes on Section 1 and nothing past it.
  • Conflating side effects and reward hacking. Both involve unintended behavior; the distinction is where the failure lives — side effects are incidental damage while pursuing the real goal honestly, reward hacking is gaming the metric itself. Worth drawing out explicitly if it comes up.
  • Treating Scalable Oversight and Distributional Shift as not fitting the pattern. They're less example-driven up front than Side Effects and Reward Hacking — flag this as a legitimate observation rather than a student error if a jigsaw group raises it. It's a good moment to reinforce that the "pattern" from Pass 2 is a heuristic, not a rule every section obeys equally.
  • Skipping the worksheet and trying to hold it all in their head. This is the whole point of making them write by hand — if a student shows up with nothing on paper, there's nothing to compare, and the exercise degrades into a lecture. Consider a light participation check on the worksheet itself.

Note for the semester arc

"Objective function" as a concept is a clean bridge to the course's principal-agent thread — the same failure modes discussed here (wrong objective, expensive-to-evaluate objective, bad behavior while learning/acting) have direct analogues in human organizations (misaligned incentives, costly monitoring, bad behavior while new employees are still learning norms). Worth a one-line callback if there's time, to start seeding the parallel before it's made explicit later in the course.
