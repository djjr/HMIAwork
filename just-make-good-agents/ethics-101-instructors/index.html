Just Make Good Agents › Ethics 101 (instructors)

This module/session introduces students to landscape and terminology of ethics. Most people have a mix of intuitions and previous exposure. We aren't trying to be exhaustive or even philosophically foundational; rather, we want to bring some order to our thinking.

Readings

Wikipedia on Ethics to get lay of the land Wallach and Vallor on virtue ethics and machines

But the main question is simple: can't the various alignment problems be solved by making agents good agents? This reframing of ethics is useful because we can see the same pattern repeating - religions and belief systems and moral education try to create better humans and we can see this as trying to take some of the burden off systems of social control and order. Build the order in and the guardrails don't need to be so sturdy. The same is true of experts and organizations (although morally good organizations are partly built through trying to have morally good human members and partly through vague ideas like "company values" - though, generically, attempts to introduce ethics in business schools is an attempt to do both). On the expert intelligence front it's interesting that we think we need a whole new level of special ethics for different types of experts (medical ethics, legal ethics, etc.).

So we start with an overview of the fields of ethics.

In everyday parlance "ethics" connotes a sort of good/bad/values mush. We want to transcend that, so we start at the top: the structure of the field itself. Metaethics asks what goodness even is — discovered or invented, universal or relative, a fact or a preference. Normative ethics asks, given some sense of the good, what you should do — consequentialism, deontology, and virtue ethics are the classic answers, each locating rightness in outcomes, rules, or character. A fourth family, contractualism and contractarianism, shifts the question slightly: instead of "what should I do," it asks "what principles could free and reasonable people agree to" — and in Rawls's hands that question stops being about one agent's choices and starts being about who legitimately gets to write the rules everyone else lives under, which is exactly the question a lab is answering, deliberately or not, every time it decides whose feedback trains a model. Applied ethics, finally, is normative theory pointed at a domain — medical ethics, engineering ethics, AI ethics, professional ethics generally. This is our synoptic starting point. The takeaway for students is fluency in moving between these levels: to look at a claim about AI ethics and recognize which normative mode it's operating in, and what metaethical stance it's quietly leaning on, even if people aren't naming it explicitly.

Exercise

We want to design the ideal roommate or robot. And we will do so by building in ethics. In one version we lean on virtue ethics and choose from among a menu of traits (within a budget - which four do you choose?). In another our approach is deontological and we select from a menu of rule systems (principles). And in the third we design consequentialist ethics into our roommate or robot by giving it a utility function to optimize.

We want to discover distinct failure modes of each approach.

  • Trait-budget agents fail by trait conflict and overfitting (e.g., maximize "honesty" alone and you get a roommate who volunteers unsolicited critique at 7am). A model that over-indexes on one RLHF-trained trait becomes sycophantic or brutally blunt. Aristotle's fix (each virtue is a mean between a deficiency and an excess — courage between cowardice and recklessness) is itself a proto-regularizer, and it's worth naming it that way. But we note that his solution was not simply "moderation in all things" - he recognizes the need for practical wisdom (phronesis) to find courage as the mean between cowardice and recklessness and adapting it to the situation (battle vs. driving).
  • Rule-based agents can fail by over compliance, loopholes, and edge cases — a robot that "must always tell the truth" starts volunteering true-but-devastating information, or a grade-grubbing student follows the rules but finds out how to maximize grades without learning. This is deontology's version of reward hacking: satisfying the letter while defeating the intent as we saw in Amodei et al.
  • Utility-maximizing agents fail by "specification gaming" (e.g., a roommate optimizing "household harmony" learns that hiding bad news scores better than honest friction) and "instrumental convergence" (e.g., top level goals converge on self preservation: the harmony-seeking robot prevents you from moving out because with no roommates there's no harmony). We can point straight back at Concrete Problems and say: computer scientists didn't discover this failure, ethicists did. Robert Nozick's utility monster, from Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974) derives so much marginal utility from resources that a strict utilitarian calculus says you should funnel everything to it at everyone else's expense - specification gaming against consequentialism, constructed by a philosopher, forty-plus years before "Concrete Problems." Then we scaffold some didactic choices:
  1. Force a principle-conflict: require a case where two principles point in opposite directions ("always be honest" rule collides with "protect your roommate's wellbeing"). Cf Asimov's Three Laws as a demonstration that any small fixed rule-set eventually needs a tiebreaker and cf. constitutional approaches in AI that specify explicit priority orderings rather than pretending conflicts won't arise.
  2. Broach metaethics with a question: "where did your list come from?" Did the trait list come from what your group already happens to value, a philosophical argument, or what you guessed a professor wants to hear? Same struggle we'll have when we ask "whose values does RLHF actually encode."
  3. Lean into the veil of ignorance: Ask who you are putting this list together? "Before you lock in your four traits or your rule list, would you accept this choice if you didn't know whether you'd be the designer or the roommate?"
  4. Ask about professional-ethics. A licensing body's code of conduct is a rule-system, like the robot's constitution — but authored collectively by a guild, enforced by social/legal sanction rather than a training loss, and revised slowly through case law and amendment. Baked into institutions is a third enforcement mechanism (beyond "baked into weights" and "baked into a training-time constitution"). A professional regime exists because the client can't fully monitor the expert, and that lack of transparency undermines our trust in the other two. It's a scalable-oversight problem.

Beat 1 — Let the analogy build itself (10 min, no jargon yet)
Don't open with "phronesis" or "IRL." Open with a case: your roommate-robot has a fixed rule ("always tell the truth") and it's failing in some new situation you didn't anticipate. Ask: what would it take for the robot to actually get this right, going forward, for the next unanticipated situation too — not just this one patched by a fixed line of code? Let students generate answers. With luck they'll converge on something like "it needs to have watched a lot of examples and picked up on what actually matters, not just memorized the rule" — that's phronesis and IRL both, arrived at from the same direction, no labels yet.

Beat 2 — Name both sides, let the parallel land explicitly
Now give them Wallach & Vallor's phronesis (skill built through habituation and particular judgment, not rule-following) side by side with a one-paragraph plain-language IRL sketch (infer the objective from observed behavior rather than hand-specify it). Ask them to write, in their own words, why these feel like the same move. Most will land on something like "both refuse to write the rule down in advance." Let that sit as a genuine win — it is a real insight, don't undercut it yet.

Beat 3 — The reveal: push on what IRL does with what it infers
Ask directly: once IRL has inferred the reward function, what happens to it? Answer: it gets optimized — argmax, same as any RL system. The inference step was upstream of specification, but the backend is still a maximization over a scalar. Then ask: does phronesis output a scalar to be maximized? This is where you want them to stall a little — good, that's the sign the disanalogy is landing. Aristotle's actual claim is stronger than "the quantity is hard to pin down": there may be no single quantity there at all, just incommensurable goods weighed in situ, no argmax waiting at the bottom once you've done enough inference.

Beat 4 — Name the general trap
State it as the takeaway, not a caveat: specification-avoidance at the front end doesn't guarantee non-optimization at the back end. IRL relocates the hard part (where does the objective come from?) without eliminating the assumption that ethical/competent behavior consists of optimizing something. Phronesis denies that assumption altogether. Worth an explicit callback here to the trait-budget/utility-function trichotomy from the roommate exercise — this is a fourth thing, not a variant of the third.

Staging notes on the two flags from before:

  • CIRL collision: in this module, stay informal and don't name CIRL specifically — use plain Ng & Russell-style IRL (single agent inferring a reward from a fixed set of demonstrations, no cooperative/shared-dynamics element). Save the actual CIRL apparatus, with its Meadian generalized-other framing, for Values Aren't Enough I. Same underlying idea (learn objectives from behavior) shows up twice, but the formal object only does technical work once — here it's just "IRL, gestured at," there it's "CIRL, built out." That should keep the two modules from fighting over the same machinery.
  • Sequencing: since this is Ethics 102, well before AI 101/102 gives them pretrain/SFT/RLHF vocabulary, keep Beat 3 verbal and example-driven — no equations, no "reward function" notation, just "the thing being maximized." You already have a plan to reintroduce this formally in Values Aren't Enough I once they have the RL scaffolding; this pass should feel like a preview they'll recognize later, not a technical explanation you're substituting for one.

One more possible payoff, if you want it: this is a clean spot to plant a seed for the Character-or-Structures deep dive months later. Phronesis-without-argmax is arguably the philosophical ancestor of your qualification-mechanism cluster (skill built through graduated trust and practical experience, not certified by a single test) — pilot's hours-in-type is a phronesis-adjacent idea, not a specification-adjacent one. A one-line callback now ("hold onto this — we'll see a very literal version of 'skill without a formula' when we get to qualification mechanisms") costs nothing and gives week 18 a discover-first hook it can cash in.
