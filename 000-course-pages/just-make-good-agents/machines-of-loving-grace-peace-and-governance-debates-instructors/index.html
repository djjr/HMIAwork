Debate Topics (AI generated first drafts)

1. Does the "entente strategy" make war more or less likely?
Amodei frames a democratic coalition racing to lock in AI advantage as the way to avoid authoritarian domination. One side can argue the strategy is prudent deterrence — clear superiority discourages conflict and gives adversaries an incentive to accept the "bargain" rather than fight. The other side can argue that an explicit race for decisive advantage is itself destabilizing — it could trigger the very great-power conflict it's meant to prevent, especially if a rival concludes it's being permanently locked out.

2. Is AI structurally neutral, or does it tilt toward one form of government?
Amodei is explicit that he sees "no strong reason" AI will structurally favor democracy the way he thinks it will favor health, unlike his confidence elsewhere in the essay. Students can argue for the propaganda/surveillance case (AI as an autocrat's tool) or against it (citing his own later claims that free information and AI-assisted dissidents could structurally erode authoritarian control) — and interrogate whether both can't be true depending on who controls the AI.

3. Should AI systems be used to help adjudicate legal and judicial decisions?
The essay proposes AI could reduce bias in law by handling "messy" real-world judgment more consistently than biased humans, provided training/interpretability are transparent. One side can defend this as a genuine improvement over documented human judicial bias; the other can argue that opaque-by-nature statistical systems making "fuzzy" legal judgments risk laundering bias behind a veneer of mechanical impartiality, with less accountability than a human judge who can be questioned, appealed, or removed.

4. Is "AI superiority as leverage" a legitimate way to spread democratic norms, or a form of coercion?
The entente strategy offers AI benefits in exchange for supporting the coalition's strategy — carrot and stick. Students can argue this is a fair, historically precedented model (the essay's own "Atoms for Peace" analogy) for extending self-government and rights. Others can argue that conditioning access to a transformative technology on political alignment is a form of coercive leverage that sits uneasily with the liberal values it claims to promote — especially for countries that aren't autocracies but simply neutral or nonaligned.

5. Should democracies prioritize winning an AI race against rivals, or prioritize broad international cooperation (including with rivals) on AI safety?
This one can be framed directly against the essay's premise. One side defends the entente logic: safety and democracy both require decisive Western/allied advantage, since a safety-focused approach that shares capability equally could hand advantage to authoritarian actors. The other side argues that treating AI development as a zero-sum geopolitical race increases the odds of corner-cutting on safety and forecloses the kind of coordinated, verifiable safety norms that might matter more than who "wins."

Instructor Notes — Debate Topics on "Peace and Governance" (Machines of Loving Grace)

Companion to the five debate topics drawn from the "Peace and governance" section of Amodei's essay. Format: assign sides, not something to hand out — students should encounter the tension cold and find the citations themselves, then check against this key.

Objective

The section is unusual among the course's readings in that Amodei himself flags his own uncertainty ("I see no strong reason to believe...," "somewhat vague ideas... may be unrealistically utopian") — so the debate exercise isn't about catching him in a contradiction, it's about inhabiting a live disagreement the author is having with himself. Each topic below has textual support on both sides, which is the point: a good answer cites the essay against itself, not just for one position.

This section also connects backward to the alignment taxonomy — Amodei is the same author as Concrete Problems — and forward to the four-strand coordination framework, since several of these topics are really instances of "which strand — markets, hierarchy, groups, shared meaning — should govern high-stakes AI coordination at civilizational scale," just without that vocabulary yet. Resist naming the framework during the debate itself; it's more useful revealed afterward as a "here's what you were actually arguing about" move than front-loaded.

Suggested flow (~50–60 min class)

Five topics is enough for either (a) one deep debate with the rest as backup/discussion, or (b) a jigsaw with five groups each taking one topic and doing a shorter defend-and-report. Given the topics vary a lot in difficulty (Topic 3 is far more concrete and gettable than Topic 1 or 5), jigsaw is probably the safer default — it stops the strongest arguers from dominating one plenary debate and lets each group calibrate to a topic's difficulty. Flagging this as a genuine judgment call, not a resolved recommendation — depends how much you want students defending a position they didn't choose (assign sides) vs. picking one they believe (self-select, then you assign side within it).

If using assigned sides: assign the counter-intuitive side deliberately at least once (e.g., have a group argue the entente strategy is destabilizing, or that AI is structurally pro-democratic) — the discomfort of arguing against your prior is a lot of the pedagogical value here, and it's the same "argue from outside your own position" muscle the roommate/robot veil-of-ignorance exercise trains.

Topic 1 — Does the "entente strategy" make war more or less likely?

The tension. Amodei presents the entente strategy as the mechanism for avoiding AI-powered authoritarian domination — but a strategy built on "gain a clear advantage," "military superiority (the stick)," and isolating adversaries is also, structurally, an arms-race posture.

Pro (stabilizing). Decisive, credible advantage removes the incentive to fight — a rival facing certain loss rationally takes the "bargain" instead (this is Amodei's own bet, and echoes real Cold War deterrence logic he's implicitly drawing on). The carrot ("distribute the benefits... to a wider and wider group of countries") is doing real work here too — it's not pure coercion, it's supposed to make defection from autocracy individually rational for more and more countries over time, shrinking the coalition that has anything to fight for.

Con (destabilizing). Arms races are exactly the mechanism that produced the two world wars Amodei cites two paragraphs earlier as evidence that "technological improvement... leading to democracy and peace" is "a very loose trend." A rival that concludes it is being permanently locked out — not offered a real bargain, just managed into irrelevance — has less to lose from a preemptive move, not more. Worth pushing students to ask: at what point does "the coalition isolates our worst adversaries" stop looking like an offer and start looking like encirclement from the other side of the table?

Discussion prompt. "Amodei cites 1900s optimism about war being 'behind us' as a cautionary example — does his own entente strategy risk the same overconfidence, or is citing the failure mode evidence he's already accounted for it?"

Pitfall to watch for. Students collapsing this into "is war good or bad" — the actual debate is about the causal mechanism (does visible superiority deter or provoke), not whether anyone wants conflict. Redirect back to the mechanism if this happens.

Topic 2 — Is AI structurally neutral, or does it tilt toward one form of government?

The tension. Amodei says outright he sees no structural reason AI favors democracy the way it favors health — then spends the rest of the section building a case (free information, an "AI Popović," countering propaganda) for why it plausibly does, conditional on who wins Topic 1's race.

Pro (AI favors autocracy). His own list: propaganda, surveillance — "major tools in the autocrat's toolkit" — plus the essay's own citation of internet technology disadvantaging democracy during the Arab Spring period, against initial expectations. This is close to a documented historical precedent for tech optimism about liberalization going the wrong way.

Pro (AI favors democracy). His later argument: repressive governments survive on controlled information ("the emperor has no clothes"), and AI that individuals can't be blocked from accessing could erode that durably, at scale, in a way past technology couldn't. Also the indirect argument — better mental health, wellbeing, and education (from earlier sections) are correlated with lower support for authoritarian leaders, so even non-political AI benefits could have political spillover.

The sharper version of this debate. Both "pro" cases are true conditionally — dual-use technology, whoever controls the frontier gets the advantage. Push students who've done both sides to notice that Amodei's actual claim isn't "AI favors democracy" or "AI favors autocracy" — it's "AI favors whoever has it," which quietly makes Topic 1 (does the entente strategy work) load-bearing for the answer to Topic 2. If a jigsaw group notices this dependency between two "separate" topics unprompted, that's worth calling out to the room.

Discussion prompt. "Is 'structurally neutral, tilts toward whoever controls it' actually a coherent third position, or is it just restating that AI is dual-use and therefore ducking the question?"

Topic 3 — Should AI systems be used to help adjudicate legal and judicial decisions?

The tension. This is the most concrete and "gettable" of the five — good one to lead with if doing a single plenary debate, since it warms the room up before the harder geopolitical ones.

Pro. Amodei's argument is specifically that law has always required humans to interpret "notoriously imprecise criteria" ("cruel and unusual punishment") and that this interpretation is where documented human bias, favoritism, and arbitrariness creep in — and that AI, unlike a "smart contract," might be the first technology capable of handling that fuzziness in a way that's actually auditable (interpretability techniques that can inspect a model "in a way that is simply not possible with humans").

Con. The auditability claim is doing a lot of work and is conditional on interpretability tooling that doesn't fully exist yet — "a mature science of AI could conceivably provide it" is a hedge worth making students notice. Opaque statistical pattern-matching dressed in the language of impartiality could be less accountable than a human judge, who can at least be questioned, cross-examined, and have their reasoning subjected to appeal in terms a citizen understands.

Course connection — this is the sharpest tie-in to scalable oversight. A judge's rulings are hard and expensive to check at scale (appeals are slow, rare, costly) — the exact structure of Amodei's own Concrete Problems scalable oversight problem, just instantiated in a courtroom instead of an RL loop. Worth an explicit callback: "we've already met this problem, just wearing a robe." Also connects to the ethics module's professional-ethics thread (a licensing body is a human-scale answer to "the client/citizen can't fully monitor the expert").

Discussion prompt. "Amodei says he isn't proposing AI replace judges, only work alongside them. Does that hedge resolve the debate, or just relocate it — who has final say when the AI's fuzzy judgment and the human judge's fuzzy judgment disagree?"

Topic 4 — Is "AI superiority as leverage" legitimate persuasion, or coercion?

The tension. The entente strategy explicitly trades AI benefits for political alignment ("in exchange for supporting the coalition's strategy to promote democracy"). Amodei's own analogy — "Atoms for Peace" — is worth having students actually look up if they don't already know it; it cuts both ways (a genuine nonproliferation/benefit-sharing regime, but also Cold War soft-power statecraft with real strings attached).

Pro (legitimate). Historical precedent for extending rights and self-government has often involved exactly this kind of conditional offer — access to a coalition's benefits in exchange for aligning with its norms (trade blocs, defense pacts, development aid). If democracies control the safest, most broadly beneficial path to advanced AI, sharing it conditionally is arguably more legitimate than either hoarding it or handing it over unconditionally to any regime.

Con (coercive). Conditioning access to a transformative general-purpose technology on political alignment sits uncomfortably with the liberal-democratic values it's supposedly promoting — those values are usually framed as chosen, not purchased. Also worth raising: what about the nonaligned middle — countries that are neither autocracies nor eager coalition members? The essay's framing (isolate adversaries, absorb everyone else) doesn't obviously leave room for principled neutrality.

Discussion prompt. "Where's the line between an incentive and a coercive bargain — does it depend on how essential the withheld thing is? Is AI more like a trade tariff or more like withholding food aid?"

Pitfall. This one is prone to sliding into a generic "is soft power good" conversation. Keep it anchored to the specific asymmetry in the text: this isn't ordinary diplomacy, it's leverage built on a technology Amodei elsewhere describes as potentially civilization-altering — ask whether that changes the ethics of conditioning access to it.

Topic 5 — Race for advantage, or prioritize broad cooperation (including with rivals)?

The tension. This is the topic that most directly contests the essay's premise, so it's the best closer if running a single debate, or the one to hand to your strongest group in a jigsaw.

Pro (race/entente). Amodei's implicit argument: safety and democratic values both require the right actors to be ahead, since a cooperative approach that shares capability evenly could just as easily hand real advantage to whichever actor is least constrained by safety norms in the first place — cooperation without differential advantage doesn't obviously produce a safer or more democratic outcome, just a faster race to the bottom shared by everyone.

Con (cooperation). Treating advanced AI as a zero-sum geopolitical race creates exactly the incentive structure — speed pressure, secrecy, distrust — that makes safety corner-cutting more likely, not less; racing dynamics are usually cited as a safety risk factor, not a solution to one. There's also a coordination argument: many of the essay's own hoped-for outcomes (verifiable safety norms, avoiding uncontrolled proliferation) look more like the sort of thing that needs broad buy-in, including from rivals, than something a race that "isolates" adversaries would tend to produce.

Course connection. This is the cleanest place in the whole set to surface the four-strand coordination framework explicitly, if you're saving that reveal for here: the "race" answer is a hierarchy/markets-flavored solution (competitive advantage, a coalition enforcing terms), the "cooperation" answer leans on shared meaning/groups (norms, verification, buy-in) — worth asking students directly, after the debate, which strand each side's argument was implicitly leaning on, and whether they'd endorsed the analogy or the strand it belongs to for the whole class period before anyone named it.

Discussion prompt. "Does Amodei's essay actually pick a side here, or does it want both — entente for capability, verified cooperation for the norms that flow from it? Is that combination coherent or does it quietly assume Topic 1's bet (superiority deters rather than provokes) already went the coalition's way?"

Sequencing note

If this slots in after the four-strand coordination module, Topic 5 becomes the natural bridge/synthesis topic and can close the session. If it runs before that module (which the discover-first principle would favor, since students would be discovering the strands experientially through disagreement rather than being handed the vocabulary first), save the "which strand was this" framing entirely for a later callback rather than raising it here — flagging this as the same sequencing trade-off already resolved for Goodhart's Law in the Concrete Problems module, so worth deciding consistently with that choice rather than independently.

Open items
