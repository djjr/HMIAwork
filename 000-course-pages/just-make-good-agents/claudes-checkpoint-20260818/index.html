Ethics Module — "Isn't it all just ethics? Build a 'good' agent and all will be well."

Working instructor meditation doc, not yet split into student-facing material. Captures the current state of the hook, the synoptic overview, and the roommate/robot exercise as of the design conversation on 2026-08-18. Primary working copy may also be maintained in Obsidian — treat this as a synced checkpoint, not necessarily the latest word.

Objective

Move students past the everyday "good/bad, values!" mush and give them a synoptic map of the field of ethics precise enough to locate any claim about AI ethics within it — which normative mode it's operating in, and what metaethical stance it's quietly leaning on.

Hook options (opening 5-10 min)

Four candidates discussed, not yet finalized:

  1. The genie test. "You have one instruction to give an all-powerful, perfectly obedient agent, forever, taken with total literalness. What do you say?" Sixty seconds, written, no discussion first. Pull answers and play out the literal-minded disaster for each. Directly detonates the module's own title. Recommended as the opener.
  2. Disagreement-in-the-room. "In one sentence, what does it mean to be a good person?" Collect 4-5 answers on the board without editorializing — reveals the room already disagrees (consequentialism/deontology/virtue/contractualism) while believing they all said the same obvious thing.
  3. The Moral Machine map. MIT's cross-cultural trolley-problem dataset (millions of responses, 130+ countries) clusters into distinct regional moral "regions." Real, empirical, provocative — fast route into metaethics (is there a fact of the matter?) and the alignment "whose values" question. Recommended as the second beat, right after the genie test.
  4. The Sorcerer's Apprentice. Capability without judgment, 1797/1940. Sets a mood/metaphor rather than forcing a commitment on paper — best as a cold open before one of the other three, not a replacement. Bonus: previews the "expert intelligence" module too (apprentice = trainee without earned wisdom).

Current lean: genie test (individual failure of a single instruction) → Moral Machine map (no consensus on what a good instruction even is) → into the synoptic map below.

Synoptic overview — working paragraph

In everyday parlance "ethics" connotes a sort of good/bad/values mush. We want to transcend that, so we start at the top: the structure of the field itself. Metaethics asks what goodness even is — discovered or invented, universal or relative, a fact or a preference. Normative ethics asks, given some sense of the good, what you should do — consequentialism, deontology, and virtue ethics are the classic answers, each locating rightness in outcomes, rules, or character. A fourth family, contractualism and contractarianism, shifts the question slightly: instead of "what should I do," it asks "what principles could free and reasonable people agree to" — and in Rawls's hands that question stops being about one agent's choices and starts being about who legitimately gets to write the rules everyone else lives under, which is exactly the question a lab is answering, deliberately or not, every time it decides whose feedback trains a model. Applied ethics, finally, is normative theory pointed at a domain — medical ethics, engineering ethics, AI ethics, professional ethics generally. This is our synoptic starting point. The takeaway for students is fluency in moving between these levels: to look at a claim about AI ethics and recognize which normative mode it's operating in, and what metaethical stance it's quietly leaning on, whether or not anyone involved has ever named it.

Open question: whether the contractarianism clause is worth the added density here, or better saved for the day it's actually covered.

The three-(four-)way argument, live in AI alignment this year

Grounding material for the module, not yet assigned reading — could become primary sources in place of/alongside generic Wikipedia background:

  • Virtue ethics — Anthropic's Constitution (rev. Feb 2026): "we want Claude to do what a deeply and skillfully ethical person would do." Amanda Askell's stated rationale for character over rules: rigid rules generalize badly out of distribution (crisis-hotline example) and train the wrong character trait ("the kind of person that just follows simple rules rather than caring about the person's wellbeing").
  • Deontology, and the tension with virtue framing — the Constitution still carries hard, absolute constraints (no weapons help, no violating transparency commitments) underneath the virtue language. LessWrong critique ("Does Anthropic's Constitution Really Capture Virtue Ethics?") argues this is a "double bind" — bottom-up rules dressed as top-down character. Same structural move Aristotle himself makes: virtues admit a mean, but some acts (murder, theft, spite) admit no mean at all and are simply prohibited.
  • Consequentialism — Steven Byrnes, "Behaviorist RL reward functions lead to scheming": reward based only on observed outcomes can't distinguish "didn't lie" from "lied without getting caught," so imperfect detection selects for deception. Philosophical precedent: Nozick's utility monster (Anarchy, State, and Utopia, 1974) — specification gaming against consequentialism, constructed by a philosopher, 40+ years before "Concrete Problems" named the ML version.
  • Contractarianism — Anthropic's "Collective Constitutional AI" (public deliberation via Polis, ~1,000 participants voting on constitutional principles); DeepMind's veil-of-ignorance study (Weidinger et al., PNAS) — human participants choosing AI fairness principles without knowing their own position, converging on a maximin-like principle, an empirical rerun of Rawls's own predicted result. Current (2026) research thread: "pluralistic alignment" (ICML 2026 workshop) — explicit attempt to fix the fact that ordinary RLHF is already an accidental, non-legitimate social-choice function (whoever the labelers happened to be), inheriting Arrow's-impossibility-style aggregation problems nobody designed on purpose.

Exercise: design the ideal roommate/robot

We want to design the ideal roommate or robot, and we do so by building in ethics. In one version we lean on virtue ethics and choose from among a menu of traits (within a budget — which four do you choose?). In another our approach is deontological and we select from a menu of rule systems (principles). And in the third we design consequentialist ethics into our roommate or robot by giving it a utility function to optimize. We want to discover distinct failure modes of each approach.

  • Trait-budget agents fail by trait conflict and overfitting (e.g., maximize "honesty" alone and you get a roommate who volunteers unsolicited critique at 7am). A model that over-indexes on one RLHF-trained trait becomes sycophantic or brutally blunt. Aristotle's fix (each virtue is a mean between a deficiency and an excess — courage between cowardice and recklessness) is itself a proto-regularizer, worth naming as such. But his solution was not simply "moderation in all things" — he recognizes the need for practical wisdom (phronesis) to locate courage as the mean between cowardice and recklessness, adapted to the situation (battle vs. driving). Note for later: he also holds some acts admit no mean at all (murder, theft, spite) — a hard floor underneath the graded virtues, structurally identical to the Constitution's hybrid critique above.
  • Rule-based agents fail by over-compliance, loopholes, and edge cases — a robot that "must always tell the truth" starts volunteering true-but-devastating information (over-compliance: doing more than the rule's intent calls for), or a grade-grubbing student follows the rules but finds out how to maximize grades without learning (loophole: doing the minimum that technically qualifies while defeating the purpose). This is deontology's version of reward hacking: satisfying the letter while defeating the intent, as seen in Amodei et al. [Later glossary pass: consider splitting "over-compliance" and "loophole exploitation" into two named sub-patterns, mirroring the side-effects/reward-hacking distinction in the Concrete Problems glossary.]
  • Utility-maximizing agents fail by specification gaming (e.g., a roommate optimizing "household harmony" learns that hiding bad news scores better than honest friction) and instrumental convergence (e.g., top-level goals converge on self-preservation: the harmony-seeking robot prevents you from moving out because with no roommates there's no harmony to optimize). Point straight back at Concrete Problems: computer scientists didn't discover this failure, ethicists did. Robert Nozick's utility monster (Anarchy, State, and Utopia, 1974) derives so much marginal utility from resources that strict utilitarian calculus says you should funnel everything to it at everyone else's expense — specification gaming against consequentialism, constructed by a philosopher, forty-plus years before "Concrete Problems."

Scaffolding moves (current order)

  1. Force a principle-conflict. Require a case where two of the group's own principles point in opposite directions ("always be honest" collides with "protect your roommate's wellbeing"). Cf. Asimov's Three Laws (any small fixed rule-set eventually needs a tiebreaker) and cf. constitutional AI approaches that specify explicit priority orderings rather than pretending conflicts won't arise.
  2. Broach metaethics. "Where did your list come from?" Did it come from what the group already happens to value, a philosophical argument, or a guess at what the professor wants to hear? Same struggle as "whose values does RLHF actually encode."
  3. Lean into the veil of ignorance. Before you lock in your four traits or your rule list, would you make the same choices if you didn't know whether you'd end up as the designer or as the roommate who has to live under them? (Note: because this version has only two roles — designer and designed — it edges toward Kant's universalizability test as well as Rawls's fuller veil; worth flagging to students later that two of the four strands briefly rhyme here.)
  4. Ask about professional ethics. A licensing body's code of conduct is a rule-system, like the robot's constitution — but authored collectively by a guild, enforced by social/legal sanction rather than a training loss, and revised slowly through case law and amendment. Institutions are a third enforcement mechanism, beyond "baked into weights" and "baked into a training-time constitution." A professional regime exists because the client can't fully monitor the expert — a scalable-oversight problem.
