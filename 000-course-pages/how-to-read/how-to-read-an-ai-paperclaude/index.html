How to Read an AI/ML Research Paper (Without Getting Lost)

*A short guide for scanning and understanding papers you'll find on arXiv.

The core idea: read for structure before you read for content

A research paper is not written in the order you're meant to consume it, and it's definitely not written in the order the authors actually did the work. It's a reconstruction — a case being made, with the strongest, most compressed version of the argument placed at the beginning (abstract) and the end (conclusion), and the supporting evidence in between. If you read a paper top to bottom, sentence by sentence, the way you'd read a novel, you'll hit a dense paragraph of math or jargon on page 3, stall out, and never learn what the paper actually claims. That's the single most common way undergrads give up on papers.

The fix is to read breadth-first, then depth-first: build a rough map of the whole argument before you commit to understanding any one part of it in detail. Once you have the map, you can decide which parts are worth the depth — and when you do go deep, you'll know where that section fits into the larger claim, instead of drowning in it with no context.

Pass 1: Read the bookends (5 minutes)

Read only the title, abstract, and conclusion. Skip everything else. The abstract is the authors' own compressed summary of the entire paper — problem, approach, and takeaway, usually in under 200 words. The conclusion is where they restate the claim now that you (hypothetically) have the evidence, often with more candor about limitations and open questions than the abstract allows.

Pass 2: Read the map (10 minutes)

Now read the introduction and every section heading, plus every figure and table caption, without reading the body paragraphs underneath them. Skip the math. Skip the related-work citations. You're not learning the content yet — you're learning the paper's skeleton: what order the ideas come in, and why that order was chosen.

Write out the skeleton of the argument based on the abstract and introduction. What will the paper do?

Then, what's the outline - just based on the section headings? Try literally writing them out. What seem to be the big ideas, the main concepts in the paper? Again, write them down.

This much will often lay out the whole paper's argument before you've read a single technical paragraph. Or, it sets you up with a task - what you want to find out about the argument. This is what "getting the flow" means: not memorizing facts, but noticing the organizing logic the sections are hung on. Most well-written papers have a single sentence or two that do this — your job on this pass is to find it.

How good an understanding of those main concepts or terms do you have? Take a moment before diving deeper to extract definitions in your own words.

Pass 3: Go deep, but choose your depth (the rest of your time)

Only now should you read section bodies closely — and not all of them. Pick one or two sections based on what you're actually trying to get out of the paper: what confused you in Pass 2, what's most relevant to what you're studying, or what seems to be doing the most argumentative work. Read that section fully, including the examples. Expect this to be hard, slow-going work.

This is the point where the depth-first trap reappears in a different form: within a section, resist the urge to fully resolve every unfamiliar term or citation the instant you hit it. Keep a running list of "look up later" items in the margin and keep moving as long as you can still follow the main thread. Stop and look something up only when not knowing it blocks you from understanding the sentence's role in the argument — not just because it's unfamiliar. Here's where the web and LLMs can help. Try prompts such as "I'm reading ____ and they say 'blah blah blah bingo blah.' My understanding of bingo is ____ am I on the right track?"

A few tactics that compound

Annotate as you go: one sentence per section (or paragraph sometimes), in your own words, either in the margin or a separate notes doc. If you can't write that sentence, you didn't actually get the section — reread the heading and topic sentence, not the whole thing again. Pay attention to hedged and comparative language ("we believe," "in contrast to prior work," "this remains an open problem") — that's where authors are telling you, explicitly, how confident they are and what's still unsettled; beginners often skim right past it while chasing technical detail. And notice what background the paper assumes and who it is written for - is it about experimental results and targeted at others who do this kind of work or is it a general position paper or a an attempt to consolidate a lot of work? The kind of paper it is vis a vis the kind of reader you are has a big effect on what constitutes a good solid read by you.
