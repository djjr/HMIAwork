AI Outline

ACCOUNTS

  • Describes the nature of accounts as statements made to explain untoward behavior and bridge the gap between actions and expectations.
  • Presents a classification of accounts by content as excuses and justifications, each with its own subtypes.
  • Discusses how excuses and justifications function as socially approved vocabularies that neutralize an act or its consequences.
  • Highlights the role of background expectations in the honoring or non-honoring of accounts.

TYPES OF ACCOUNTS

  • Defines excuses as socially approved vocabularies for mitigating or relieving responsibility when conduct is questioned.
  • Lists four modal forms of excuses: appeal to accidents, appeal to defeasibility, appeal to biological drives, and scapegoating.
  • Defines justifications as accounts in which one accepts responsibility for the act in question, but denies the pejorative quality associated with it.
  • Mentions several types of justifications, including denial of injury, denial of victim, condemnation of condemners, appeal to loyalties, sad tales, and self-fulfillment.

HONORING ACCOUNTS, AND BACKGROUND EXPECTATIONS

  • Explains how accounts may be honored or not honored, and the role of background expectations in this process.
  • Discusses how the character of the social circle, cultural norms, and situational context influence the honoring of accounts.
  • Notes that the inability to invoke situationally appropriate accounts can be taken as a sign of mental illness.
  • Highlights the role of appearance in crediting or discrediting accounts.

LINGUISTIC STYLES AND ACCOUNTS

  • Presents five linguistic styles that frame the manner in which an account will be given: intimate, casual, consultative, formal, and frozen.
  • Discusses how these styles are ordered on a scale of decreasing social intimacy and how they relate to the social circle in which they are employed.
  • Explains how the appropriateness of the linguistic style can influence the acceptance or refusal of an account.
  • Notes that idiomatic errors in an account can provide an opportunity for the hearer to dishonor the speaker.

STRATEGIES FOR AVOIDING ACCOUNTS

  • Discusses how the vulnerability of actors to questions concerning their conduct varies with the situation and the status of the actors.
  • Presents three strategies for avoiding accounts: mystification, referral, and identity switching.
  • Explains how these strategies function and the circumstances under which they are likely to be employed.

NEGOTIATING IDENTITIES, AND ACCOUNTS

  • Discusses how accounts always occur between persons in roles and how the identities of the interactants are negotiated as part of the encounter.
  • Explains how identity assumption and "alter-casting" are prerequisites to the presentation of accounts.
  • Notes that the particularistic or universalistic identity of the interactants can influence the manner and outcome of the account situation.
  • Highlights the phased nature of accounts and how one account can generate the question that gives rise to another.

CONCLUSION

  • Emphasizes the importance of studying talk as a distinctive feature of humans.
  • Calls for further research on the background expectations that determine the range of acceptable accounts.
  • Suggests that future research on accounts should focus on the speech community as a unit of analysis.
  • Notes the intrinsic relationship between the study of deviance and the study of accounts.

A SCALE FOR DEVELOPMENTAL PROCESSES

  • Presents a new scaling procedure appropriate for developmental processes, which allows for the acquisition and dropping of traits in a specific order.
  • Discusses item ordering and provides two indicators of the scale's utility: an index of scalability and a significance test.
  • Provides two illustrations of the scale using data on the independence of wives and family recreation patterns.
  • Concludes that the developmental scale is well-suited to describing these data, while Guttman scales are inadequate.
