WZ

 I believe that what Lepore and Caputo primarily disagree about is what the word “constitution”means.

Caputo’s essay focuses on functionality over legitimacy. Caputo defines the core of both human constitutions and AI constitutions as “documents written in natural language that use high-level principles and granular rules to structure an entity and provide means to evaluate the acceptability of conduct”. His following ideas are built around the definition: how the AI constitutions are, by the functions and the public impact, sufficient or not as general alignment rules. Legitimacy of the rules is yet to be improved, but it is not necessarily a criteria in defining the constitutional identity.

Lepore does not merely consider “constitution” as only the rules themselves and how the rules operate. The reading is both about the written constitution, and the surrounding supervision and revision mechanisms, which is, in a more general sense, what should belongs to the discussion of“constitutionalism”. This is why Lepore finds unsatisfactory about the existing AI constitutions: they are indeed strict written rules, and are functional in alignment, but are not accompanied by institutional mechanism and public supervision that will guarantee the accountability in a long term. From Lepore’s perspective, legitimacy should not be a separate problem when questioning whether the AI constitutions are valid.

The strongest objection to my view: Caputo actually admits the insufficiency of effective public supervision on AI constitutions. Instead of saying that the disagreement is about defining constitutions, the real difference could be how they talk from different stages of the same problem.

In my opinion, I believe Lepore and Caputo are disagreeing primarily about what makes a constitution legitimate. Lepore argues that constitutions must be talked over, debated on, and get input from the people as part of democratic participation that the constitution is governing (in this case, the constitution being AI's constitution, and the people it governs being its users). However, in her essay, she points out that the current versions of an AI constitution are primarily only written by the companies themselves, and that it's hard to get a representative sense of the people: "It wasn’t as promising as it sounds. 'The problem was, it’s not a clean experiment,' Ganguli said. 'Who are ‘the people’? What platform do we use to get their views? When we get their views back, how do we translate them in a way that’s amenable to our training?'" (pg. 8, 9). She primarily points out that the current constitutions are inherently illegitimate. Caputo, on the other hand, is much more optimistic and shifts the focus of the constitutions from rules that govern the relationship between user and agent to the fundamental rules the agent should believe "Notably, the constitutions provide a framework for AIs to think about these structures and the requirements that they impose, and how to resolve conflicts among them" (pg. 5). As such, from this focus, he believes that AI constitutions are much more achievable and attainable in his definition of legitimate. The strongest objection to my view would be that the two authors are using two different definitions of legitimate, therefore not being fair to compare the two. However, personally I feel that both definitions are partly correct in defining legitimacy as constitutions require (and affect) both of their definitions.

I think Lepore and Caputo are mainly disagreeing about what the word "constitution" means. More specifically, they disagree about whether legitimacy is part of the definition. They actually agree on most of the facts: both say Claude's constitution was written by a small private group with no public authorization, and that the people it governs have no role in ratifying or amending it. Where they split is what those gaps mean. Lepore treats them as disqualifying, so for her the document isn't a constitution at all. Caputo treats them as flaws in a document that is still a constitution.

Lepore's view shows up most clearly when she quotes Chemerinsky, who says "constitution" signifies "a popularly written and ratified document that limits what a government can do." On that definition, public authorship and ratification are built into the word itself, so Claude's document fails before you even look at what it does.

Caputo admits the same problems. He says the documents are "neither created through public processes nor subject to public oversight." But he still argues that being real constitutions doesn't mean they "possess constitutional legitimacy." For him, a document is a constitution if it creates an entity, constrains its conduct, and ranks norms. Authorization and ratification go into a separate category: legitimacy.

The strongest objection to my view is that part of the disagreement might be about what these documents actually do, not just definitions. Lepore, through Berman, says it's "impossible to see how it works," meaning how the principles turn into behavior. She also points out that the document constrains the AI but not Anthropic's own power. If she doubts it works as a constraint at all, she would reject Caputo's functional test even on his own terms. That would make the debate partly about facts, not only about the meaning of the word.

RB Lepore and Caputo primarily disagree about what the word “constitution” means and whether constitutions are innately good at fulfilling their own definitions.

Lepore thinks a constitution is the framework of rules designed to govern and protect those it governs. A constitution, in his eyes, is required to be created and amended with the input of the governed. Lepore doesn't think of Claude's constitution as a constitution, even going so far as to put scare quotes around "constitution" in parts where he questioned its legitimacy. It's also important to note that Lepore, based on the way they describe the U.S. Constitution as failing, doesn't have much faith in constitutions to do what they promise. This is going to restrict their definition of a constitution because if they already don't trust constitutions, they're going to be conservative when deciding what flaws they allow in a constitution. Under this definition, Lepore would definitely consider the constitutions of democratic republics as constitutions, but I'd also think he might label the student conduct handbook as a form of constitution.

Caputo defines a constitution as documents used to shape those subject to its behaviour. We see this when he says that "The primary role of the AI constitutions is as technical documents used for training", meaning that constitutions are defined by the results they produce, not by what the document actually contains. Caputo sees Claude's constitution as a collection of principles that guides the AI's actions. Under this definition, Caputo might consider religious texts as the constitution of a faith.

A strong objection to this view could simply be that they disagree more about what AI constitutions actually do rather than what the word "constitution" means. Since what AI constitutions do affects how people understand constitutions, and people's idea of what a constitution means affects how they write AI constitutions, you can very easily make the argument that I have the primary cause of disagreement backwards.
