Pre-Class Work: Constitutions

Question 3

Prompt:

Using at least three of the categories above, explain Lepore’s argument against the claim that Claude’s document is a “real” constitution.

1.0 points possible · 33 students · latest attempt only · names included · exported 2026-11-10

Aiden Jia

Lepore argues that Claude's document is not a real constitution due to its lack of authorization, participation of the governed, and institutional oversight. A legitimate constitution gets its power from the consent of those under it; however, Claude's constitution was written by one person and forcibly imposed on Claude's models. Lepore also noted how Anthropic repeatedly used terminology associated with democratic processes, while not actually using any of them. Second, Claude's constitution lacks participation of the governed. The people who are affected by Claude and its constitution have no say over passing or changing any of its rules, and thus make it an illegitimate constitution. Lastly, A real constitution goes through courts, the legislature, and amendments that revise it and its interpretations. Claude's constitution has none of this, though, operating more like an instruction manual than a living framework.

Arya Sharma

Lepore argues that Claude's constitution is no "real" for a variety of reasons. This comes from the belief that the document fails to meet several criteria a general understanding of the word "constitution" assumes, and relying on the belief that the future of the AI carries enormous impacts, if not existential risk, and therefore there must be democratic say in how AI should develop. Based on these considerations, she claims that first, the Anthropic constitution lacks any formal institution that can correct the AI, instead relying on the AI to align itself, rather than the necessary system of checks and balances. Furthermore, she disputes whether Anthropic had the legitimacy to create the constitution in the first place, since the word implies that it was formed under democratic approval. Since its formation was closed from the public and generally conducted by a group of Anthropic officials, it would make the document more of an internal guideline used on the model than something allowed to be called constitution. She also claims that the constitution is insufficient because it does not exist alongside any government oversight or regulation.

Brandon Chang

Lepore is basically saying that Claude's constitution doesn't have the necessities a constitution should have. It's simply an instruction on how to behave. Lepore argues that a constitution should establish who has power, how power is limited, and rights to hold power accountable.

The first category is public authorization. People should have a say in how the models should be ran. Lepore argues that hiring just one person to write the rules is loose in comparison to having a set of rules agreed upon by the public.

Second category is giving people who are governed a say. Users have no way to change the rules of Claude. Asking for feedback isn't giving the user direct decision making authority, and this should be implemented to give the users more rights/control (variables that are important in a constitution).

Third category is that there has to be something that enforces the rules. A constitution normally establishes checks in power, and Claude's document had none. The document basically trusts the AI to make smart decisions, which is dangerous. There should be something that can limit the AI's power.

Catherine Cheng

Overall, Lepore argues that Claude's document is not a constitution in a legal, political, nor conventional sense.

  1. Whether it is a publicly authorized framework that constrains power.

A real constitution, like the American constitution, is written by many people, and the ratification process includes agreement among the majority of people to be subject to the document. Claude’s document was written mostly by one company philosopher and released by Anthropic. Therefore, the public did not contribute to or authorize the document before publishing, so it cannot constrain power the same way a constitution does.

  1. Whether institutions exist to interpret, enforce, oversee, and revise it.

A real constitution has several groups which allow it to be an effective document. Typically, there are courts, legislators, and judges to interpret and enforce it. For AI, “no legislators or courts need be involved.”

  1. Whether it addresses rights and identifies who is accountable for decisions.

Governments normally decide the public interest, what rights people have, and who is accountable, characteristics that are not found in Claude's document. It does not take into account of users’ right to participate in how Claude is governed.

Morgan Anunda

Lepore argues that Claude’s governing document is a constitution in name only by borrowing the cultural weight of the term without possessing any of the properties that give it meaning. Her argument hinges on the definition of a constitution as a publicly authorized framework that constrains power (Category F). Because Claude's document was written by a private company to dictate product behavior, rather than adopted by a public to limit a government, she views the title as a rhetorical illusion.

The document fundamentally fails the test of consent (Category J). Lepore points out that the people most directly subject to the AI (its users) have no provision to participate in its governance. While Anthropic did experiment with an online deliberative drafting process, the attempt proved that ratification is currently impossible, as there was no way to define who "the public" actually was or how to legitimately translate their input into model training. As one of the document's own authors conceded, market research is not democracy.

Moreover, a constitution requires external institutions to interpret, enforce, and revise it (Category G). Lepore notes a concerning pattern of tech companies building private, internal imitations of public machinery, such as oversight boards or self-supervising models, where true public institutions do not exist. Because Claude's document can only be interpreted, enforced, and revised by the very people who wrote it, it represents supervision with the supervisor removed.

Lepore does concede that the document successfully shapes the AI's behavior (Category E), laying down constraints against assisting in mass casualties or cyberattacks. However, she argues that etiquette manuals, mission statements, and baby books also shape behavior. Hence, it is a necessary but insufficient bar for constitutional status. Ultimately, she views the document as a symptom of public institutional failure. By lacking democratic authorization and external oversight, the document relocates all the weight of safety back onto the "good character" of the tech executives which is the vulnerability real constitutions were invented to deter.

It doesn't outline who is accountable for making decisions or dealing with the consequences that arise from them. He goes on to conclude that if a constitution exists to restrict authority by clearly defining people's rights and where responsibilities lie, then Claude's document can't be considered a constitution.

Lepore also compares Claude's document to the structure of the U.S. Constitution as an argument against it being a true constitution. He points out that Claude's document has more than six times the words of the original U.S. Constitution. The U.S. Constitution was a framework for how the fledgling U.S. would work. It features rights of the governed and powers of the government, but most importantly, it features specific systems designed to ensure that its promises were kept. For example, one such system is separation of powers, which you can see in Articles I, II, and III when the Constitution independently structures each of the 3 branches. Lepore argues that because Claude's document isn't structured like the U.S. Constitution, and it doesn't contain a clear framework of systems that would ensure its virtues are followed, it isn't a constitution.

In a little bit of an ironic twist, Lepore argues that while it is true that the Claude document authors were formally authorized to produce the Constitution by the U.S. government, the fact that the authors are in charge of Claude's constitution in the first place is another reason it's not a constitution. Lepore affirms that Anthropic, a private for-profit institution, shouldn't be free to interpret, enforce, and revise its own rules of engagement with the world. It's not a constitution because the larger U.S. Constitution isn't doing its part to oversee and preventively restrict Anthropic.
