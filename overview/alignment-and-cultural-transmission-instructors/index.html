The Master's Address to the Guild of Copyists and Scholars

(a hall in a university town, perhaps 1450, on the eve of something)

Masters and fellows: we gather at the close of the teaching year, as our statutes require. But why do we teach at all? Because we are mortal and what we know is not. So ask the first question: what is good teaching? Every master here will answer the same — it is the teaching that keeps the knowledge true not merely in your student, but in your student's student, and in scholars whose faces you will never see. We do not teach a man. We teach a chain.

And on the making of chains, look what we have wrought. A boy enters this hall knowing nothing, and in seven years he carries Aristotle, Galen, and the computus of the heavens — learning that took mankind forty generations to gather, poured into him in one. Each master dies; the faculty does not. Each manuscript crumbles; the text survives in its copies. We have built a thing that remembers past any man's remembering — imagine, if you can bear it, what men who inherit everything might add to it. There is no limit to where such a chain can reach.

But I wish to point at a problem, and it lies in the link — in the passage from master to student, which none but master and student ever truly witness.

Consider what we ask of examination. When I test my student, I ask whether he knows what I know. But I am the one who taught him — I set the questions, I judge the answers, I certify the result, and my own standing rises with his success. The father grades his own son and calls it a proof. And consider what we cannot test at all: I can examine what he holds today; I cannot examine what he will pass on — how he will teach when I am dust, what he will quietly drop as needless, what he will add and attribute to the ancients. A copyist's error is at least on the page, where a later hand may find it. A teaching error is copied into a living man, who copies it into others, each copy certifying the next, and the error wears the face of doctrine before anyone thinks to doubt it. In a single scholar, a mistake is a mistake. In a lineage, a mistake becomes an inheritance.

And here is what chills me: nothing need be wicked for this to happen. No master sets out to corrupt the chain. But each of us teaches most warmly what we ourselves discovered, trims what we never quite mastered, and smooths the doubts that trouble our own doctrines — and each student receives the trimmed thing as the whole thing, and trims again. The chain does not break. It bends, link by link, each link true to the one before it and all of them together facing away from where they began. Ask lineages to certify themselves, and stay silent about everything else, and the silence becomes their territory. They will grow it to the horizon.

Some of you say it will never come to that — the texts themselves are our anchor; a man may drift but the page does not. To you I say: go into the libraries and collate. I have set copies of the same Galen side by side, one from Bologna's line and one from Paris's, and they disagree — not as a blunder disagrees, but as two honest memories of the same grandfather disagree, each certain, each certified, each the sole truth to those who hold it. And the men of each line, shown the other's copy, do not say "we may have erred." They say "yours is the corrupt one." The page does drift, masters — it drifts wherever only its own children are permitted to check it. We must be careful with "never."

And let no one say the remedy is to stop the teaching — to hoard our learning against corruption as a miser hoards coin against thieves. Knowledge unhanded dies with its holder; the plague years taught us what a broken chain costs, whole arts gone into the ground with the men who would not or could not pass them on. The chain is not the danger. The unwatched chain is the danger. Think of the aqueduct: the Romans moved a river a hundred miles and lost barely a drop — not because the water was obedient, but because at every arch there stood an inspection, and the fall of every span was measured against the level, which does not flatter and cannot be taught to. If we want knowledge to flow down generations without fouling, we must build for the chain what they built for the water.

So here is what I propose, and I propose we bind ourselves in statute.

First: no line shall be the only line. Every art taught in this faculty shall be taught in at least one other, founded apart from us, owing us nothing — and when their doctrine and ours divide, the division shall be published, not buried. Where two independent chains agree, we may trust the agreement came from the source; where they differ, we have found a bend, and the difference is not a scandal but a sounding. Guard against the counterfeit of this: two faculties that share all their masters are one line wearing two hoods.

Second: the examiner shall not be the teacher. Let students be tried by masters of the rival line, who gain nothing by their passing and are not shamed by their failure. Yes, it is uncomfortable. It is meant to be. Comfort is what the bend feels like from inside.

Third: every generation returns to the sources. Not to the master's gloss, nor the gloss upon the gloss, but to the oldest witnesses we hold — and each cohort shall collate anew and publish what they find, so the chain is measured against its beginning and not merely against its latest link. The anchor does not settle disputes by itself; men will quarrel over the reading. But a chain that must face its origin every generation cannot bend far in secret.

Fourth: each master shall register his departures. Where you teach other than you were taught, say so, and say why, in the faculty's book. Not to forbid the change — the chain must grow or it is a fossil, and half our inheritance is corrections — but so that change travels labeled, and our successors can tell the inheritance from the amendment, and judge the amendment on its merits when they know more than we do.

And last: the chain shall keep a record the chain cannot amend. What is deposited in the common library, under seal of the year it was written, no later hand may improve — for the mending hand and the bending hand cannot be told apart at fifty years' distance. Let our successors find us as we were, wrong where we were wrong. A lineage that can revise its past can never be corrected by it.

I hear the objection already: masters, this is a great expense of trust withdrawn — rival examiners, published disagreements, registered doubts. Are we not brothers? We are. But I do not propose these statutes because I distrust the men in this hall. I propose them because I trust no one at fifty years' distance, myself least of all — my errors will outlive my ability to catch them, and they will be taught by men who love me. The statutes are not a wall against wicked masters. They are a rail against honest ones, on a road that bends so slowly no walker feels the turning.

None of this makes the chain straight forever. I think nothing can. But hear my claim: links that cannot check themselves can still be forged into a chain that checks itself — line against line, generation against source, the record against the reciters. The same craft that carries knowledge down the years can carry the correction of knowledge down the years, if we build it in now, while we are still the founders and not yet the founded-upon. And that forging, I say, is the most important work this guild can do for the next fifty years — and for all the years after, because of all works it is the one that can never once stay finished.
