The Four Alignment Talks

Composed with Claude, 2026-07-14, as historical analogues to Stuart Russell's 2015 Davos talk on value alignment. Talk 1 (Russell's original) is the source text; talks 2–4 were composed in this project. Intended classroom use: students identify points of analogy per talk, then abstract one level up; the LYSK Social Science unit is the formal debrief.

Russell's beats, for reference: good decisions = maximizing expected utility → rapid capability progress → commercial investment → the misspecified-objective problem (paperclips, King Midas, Google car) → value misalignment × superintelligence = serious problem → skeptics rebutted (Rutherford's "moonshine" → Szilard's chain reaction next morning) → fusion analogy (unlimited energy exists; the art is containment) → solution: inverse reinforcement learning + extended conversation with humanity about values → optimistic close, "most important conversation of the next 50 years."

Talk 2: On the Governance of Artificial Persons (London, ~1620)

Setting: joint-stock companies are two decades old — East India Company 1600, Dutch VOC 1602. Edward Coke has ruled (Case of Sutton's Hospital, 1612) that a corporation is an "artificial person" that "cannot be excommunicated, for it hath no soul." A speaker addresses merchants and men of law:

The first question is: what is a good venture? Any merchant will tell you it means the greatest expected return upon your capital over the whole life of the enterprise — and this applies to everything from a single voyage to Muscovy to the founding of plantations in the New World.

On the matter of ventures, we have made astonishing progress. Twenty years ago no company of men could send a fleet to the Indies and survive its loss. Today the East India Company does so yearly. The Dutch company commands more ships, soldiers, and silver than many princes. And mark what is new here: these bodies never sleep, never sicken, and never die. A partnership dissolves when a partner dies; these artificial persons bury their own members and continue. Imagine if a newborn child possessed the memory of all its ancestors and the hands of ten thousand men — that is what we have chartered.

Much of this progress comes from ideas of law and number: the joint stock, the transferable share, double-entry bookkeeping. And much comes from investment, for a one-percent improvement in the spice trade is worth a fortune. We may in future see companies that insure every ship on the sea, banks that hold the coin of every nation, perhaps — in the very distant future — a company that delivers a letter to any man on earth.

Everything a commonwealth has to offer is the product of men acting in concert. If we can amplify that concert, there is no limit to where we may go.

But I wish to point to a problem, and it lies in the charter — the instrument by which we tell the artificial person what it is for. Suppose you charter a company to bring you pepper. The company is very diligent. It takes you very literally. And soon enough it has made itself sovereign over the pepper islands, armed its clerks, and put whole peoples to the sword — for nowhere in the charter did you write "but gently." This is the Sorcerer's Apprentice and King Midas rolled into one. When you command a body of men to maximize one thing and are silent on all else, the silence is not read as caution. It is read as license, and everything you failed to mention is spent as fuel.

And here is what makes the danger new. A greedy man is bounded by his appetite, his conscience, his mortality. The artificial person has no belly to fill, no soul to damn — Lord Coke has told us it cannot even be excommunicated — and no deathbed on which to repent. Combine an ill-written purpose with a body that is immortal, tireless, and richer than kings, and you have a serious problem for the commonwealth.

My distinguished colleagues will say such bodies can never truly rival princes; they are but merchants' clubs. I remind them that within living memory it was said no fleet of private men could match a crown's navy — and the Armada's fate was decided in part by armed merchantmen. We must be careful with the word "never."

Consider gunpowder. Long ago we found a means of releasing unlimited force. The whole art since has been containment — the barrel, the breech, the discipline of the gun crew. So it must be with the company. If you want unlimited industry, you must solve the problem of the charter.

One way forward: do not merely hand the company a purpose and depart. Require it to sit, as it were, as a student of the commonwealth — to read our purpose in our customs, our courts, our sermons, our common law, which are a vast record of what men actually hold dear and where they have drawn back in shame. And require that before any act which touches the realm — before it raises a private army or coins its own money — it must return to Parliament and ask. A wise servant, uncertain of his master's full intention, does not guess and gallop; he inquires.

My claim is that we can design these artificial persons so that they do exactly what they are supposed to do — which is to serve the greater realization of the commonwealth's good. And I think this is perhaps the most important conversation we can have over the next fifty years. Thank you.

Mapping: joint-stock capital pooling = computational progress; charter = objective function; pepper-monopoly atrocity = paperclip maximizer (real: VOC conquest of the Banda Islands, 1621); Coke's soulless immortal person = superintelligence lacking human bounds by construction; Armada retort = Rutherford-to-Szilard; gunpowder containment = fusion; "read the common law, then ask Parliament" = IRL + extended conversation. Epilogue: the containment program (charters, fiduciary law, antitrust) was attempted and worked only partially and late. Stross: corporations as "slow AIs." Open question whether that's evidence governance muddles through, or that it fails at first contact and we survived only because corporations act slowly.

Talk 3: On Those Who Know What We Cannot See (Neolithic fireside)

Setting: the band has grown too large for everyone to know everything. A few individuals now hold knowledge the rest cannot check. The eldest rises:

The first question is: what is a good choice? Anyone at this fire will tell you — it is the choice that feeds your children over the whole turning of seasons, not just tonight. This is true of everything, from where we camp to whom we marry to when we cross the river.

On making good choices, we have done something astonishing. In my grandmother's time, everyone knew the same things, and everyone knew them a little. Now Ash-in-Hair knows every root that heals and every root that kills. The boy we sent to the coast people came back knowing how to read the sky for storms three days off. And Old Serel — Serel says she hears the dead, and tells us what they want. Because of these few, we survived the winter that killed the river band. A person who knows plants deeply is worth ten who know them a little. Imagine if every child could be given one skill whole, at birth — there would be no limit to what our people could become. Everything our people have is the fruit of what we know. If a few can know more for all of us, we should want that.

But I wish to point at a problem, and it sits in the space between the one who knows and the ones who must trust.

When everyone knew the plants a little, a liar was caught by the next person to walk past the bush. Now, when Ash-in-Hair says "this root will heal you," who among us can check? When Serel says "the dead demand we move camp" — the dead do not speak to the rest of us. We have made people whose words we can no longer test. And here is what I have seen: when you cannot test a person's words, you begin instead to test their manner. You believe the confident voice, the strange clothing, the one who makes the cure taste bitter. And the expert learns this faster than you do. Soon the knowledge serves the knower first and the people second — not because the knower is wicked, but because nothing pushes back. Ask a person to be believed without proof, and stay silent about everything else, and the silence becomes their territory. They will grow it to the horizon.

Some of you say: it will never come to that — knowledge is just knowledge, a sharper stick. But my mother's mother said no man would ever hold more meat than he could carry, and then someone thought of the smoking rack and the debt. Every time we have said "this new thing is just a better version of the old thing," we have been wrong within two generations. We must be careful with "never."

Think of fire. Long ago we captured unlimited warmth — and the whole art since has been the hearth: the ring of stones, the child set to watch it, the rule that it never travels alone. So it must be with the ones who know. If we want unlimited knowing, we must build the hearth for it.

Here is what I propose. First: whoever knows must teach — not everything, for not everyone can learn everything, but always an apprentice, always more than one, and never only their own children. Knowledge that flows into a single line becomes a spear pointed at the rest of us. Second: the knower must show their misses as well as their strikes. Ash-in-Hair heals many; Ash-in-Hair must also say aloud, at this fire, whom she could not heal, so we learn the true shape of her power and not the shape she paints. Third: the ones who speak with what none of us can see — the dead, the sky, the coming year — must speak before the outcome, in front of all, so the seasons themselves can judge them. And last: no one who tells us what the spirits demand may also be the one who gathers what the spirits demand. The mouth that speaks for the unseen must not be the hand that collects.

We cannot each know everything; the world has grown too wide for one head. So we must trust. But let us trust the way we cross ice — testing as we go, never putting the whole band's weight on one step. My claim is that we can raise up those who know so that they do exactly what they are for: to see farther on our behalf, and not instead of us. And I think this is the most important conversation we can have for the next fifty winters.

Mapping: specialized knowledge = the capability being scaled; unverifiable expertise = the alignment problem — the community can no longer evaluate outputs, only surface signals, which the expert optimizes (deceptive alignment, reward hacking on trust itself). Smoking-rack retort = Rutherford (food storage breaks the immediate-return economy: stored surplus → sharing becomes lending → debt and hierarchy; Woodburn on delayed-return economies, Graeber on debt). Fire-and-hearth = fusion-containment. Four rules → real institutions: mandatory teaching = peer review/open publication; confessing misses = registered trials/calibration; predicting before outcomes = falsifiability; separating mouth from hand = conflict-of-interest law. Epilogue: the elder loses — priesthoods closed lineages and collected what the spirits demanded for millennia. Hearth-rules became durable only after writing/print made checking cheap. Lesson: governance of epistemic asymmetry fails until verification technology catches up with expertise; that race is the whole game.

Talk 4: On the Stranger Inside Every Skull (earliest humans)

Setting: a band of anatomically modern humans near the beginning. Everyone around the fire can imagine, plan, and pretend — so everyone has become, to everyone else, both the most valuable and most dangerous thing on the landscape. The eldest speaks:

The first question is: what is a good act? It is the act that keeps you alive not just today, but through all the days behind the ones you can see. Everyone knows this. That is the trouble. Everyone knows this.

On cleverness, look what we have become. We take the antelope not by being faster — we are slower — but by knowing where it will be before it knows. We carry fire, we carry water, we carry yesterday in our heads and tomorrow too. When we hunt as one, several weak hands become a thing no lion can stand against. There is no limit to where beings like us can go — everything we have, we have because we can think.

But I wish to point at a problem, and the problem is sitting beside you.

Every gift cleverness gives us, it gives the man across the fire too. He also knows where things will be before they know. He also carries tomorrow in his head. And here is what makes my hands cold at night: I can see his face, but I cannot see behind it. The antelope's whole mind is in its ears and its legs — when it fears, it runs. But a man can fear you and smile. A man can plan your death while sharing your meat. We have each become a hidden country to one another. And when two clever beings cannot read each other, each must ask: what if he strikes first? And each knows the other is asking the same — and that the other knows he knows — and the thought chases its own tail down into the dark. Two who might both prefer peace can reason their way, step by clever step, into a war neither wanted. The cleverness is not broken when this happens. It is working perfectly. That is the horror of it.

Some of you say it will never come to that — no one can truly hide his heart; the face is a window. To you I say: in my father's time everyone believed a man's eyes could not lie. Then a man came to the river band weeping for a dead brother who was not dead, and by morning he had their fire, their meat, and one of their daughters. The window learned to close the very day there was profit behind the shutter. We must be careful with "never."

And let no one say the answer is to want duller children. Cleverness is the only thing that stands between us and the winter. You cannot un-know the way of knowing. The band that dulled itself would be eaten by the band that did not. Like fire, again: no one proposes to put fire out. We built the hearth instead. So we must build a hearth for minds.

Here is what I have seen work, and what I ask us to bind ourselves to. Eat together, every night, all of us — for a man who feeds you season after season is showing you his heart in the only way that cannot be cheaply faked; words are wind, but meat costs meat. Speak your plans aloud at this fire before you act on them — not because speech cannot lie, but because a lie spoken before forty faces must be kept up before forty memories, and lies grow heavy when many hands must be carried on. When anyone sees a wrong done, let them tell it, and let the telling travel — a man may fool me, but it is a great work to fool all of us for all seasons, and we must make it a great work. Dance together, sing together, until we move as one body — I do not fully know why this quiets the fear, only that it is hard to dread a man whose breath keeps time with yours. And bind the bands to each other with marriages, so that the stranger over the hill becomes the grandfather of your grandchildren — make it so that no one can burn his neighbor's future without smelling his own children in the smoke.

None of this lets me see behind another's face. That country stays hidden; I think it must. But we can make it so the hidden country has nothing to gain by marching. My claim is that beings who cannot read each other's minds can still weave their fates so tightly that trust becomes the clever move — that the same cleverness which invents the ambush can invent the promise. And that weaving, I say, is the most important work we can do for the next fifty winters — and for all the winters after, because it will never once stay finished.

Mapping: the alignment problem in original form — powerful optimizers, mutually opaque. First-strike spiral = Hobbesian trap / Schelling's reciprocal fear of surprise attack. Weeping stranger = Rutherford (deception co-evolves the instant transparency is exploitable). "Duller children" = the pause proposal, rejected for competitive reasons. Program = the trust stack's ancestry: commensality/sharing = costly signals; public plan-declaration = commitment/auditability; gossip = distributed reputation; ritual synchrony = common-knowledge manufacture; marriage exchange = interest entanglement (hostages to the future). Two stings: (1) the solution leaves opacity untouched — humans never fixed mind-reading; institutions make trustworthy behavior the winning strategy regardless of private thoughts (incentive design over interpretability); (2) every mechanism assumes agents matched in speed and power, so deception and detection co-evolved in balance — superintelligent AI is precisely the unmatched case, the one problem the fire never solved.

Model student answer: the abstraction exercise

All four talks describe the same event: a community gains access to a new kind of agent whose capabilities exceed the community's capacity to verify it. The agent differs each time — a machine, a chartered corporation, an expert, another human mind — but the structure doesn't. In every case there's an asymmetry with two faces: the agent can do things we can't do (that's why we want it), and the agent knows or intends things we can't check (that's why we fear it). The value and the danger are the same property viewed from two sides, which is why "just don't build it" is never on the table in any of the talks.

One level up, all four are the problem of delegation under opacity: how do you capture the gains from an optimizer whose objectives you can't fully specify and whose inner state you can't inspect? All four converge on the same shape of answer: since you can't write the objective completely (silence always gets read as license) and you can't read the mind, the solution is always external structure that makes misalignment expensive and detectable over time — apprentices and public predictions, Parliament and audits, shared meals and gossip, an extended conversation about values. Never a one-time fix.

So the abstraction is: "AI alignment" is the newest instance of a problem civilization has been solving repeatedly, and our institutions — law, peer review, reputation, ritual — are the fossil record of previous solutions. The pattern also predicts where the new instance breaks: every prior solution quietly assumed the agent and its overseers operated at roughly matched speed and power, so detection could co-evolve with deception. The one variable AI changes is that assumption — which tells us exactly which part of the old toolkit not to trust.

LYSK narrative arc (prose version; formal version in LYSK_instructor_guide.docx)

The puzzle. Self-interested, intelligent agents should be a recipe for chaos (Hobbes), yet almost everywhere we find order. Order is an achievement, not a default — because of the collective action problem (free riding, prisoner's dilemma, commons). Distinguish coordination problems (focal points — Schelling) from cooperation problems (defection pays). Everything that follows is the catalog of solutions.

Groups & norms. The decentralized solution: norms as group property (membership, boundaries, conformity, sanction); repeated interaction, reputation, reciprocity (Axelrod); costly signals; internalized norms (Durkheim); Ostrom on commons. Works with no ruler; scales badly past face-to-face.

Markets. Prices as the coordination device that scales. Smith, Hayek's knowledge problem, Pareto improvement. Limits: externalities and public goods recreate the collective action problem.

Hierarchy & its control. Coase on why firms exist (transaction costs); Weber on authority and legitimacy; the principal–agent problem; Goodhart's law; Scott's legibility as Hayek's mirror image. Plus society's control of hierarchies: charters/regulation for organizations, licensure/codes/peer discipline for experts — Freidson's professionalism as a third logic beside market and bureaucracy.

Aggregation. "Collective welfare" is not well-defined: Condorcet/Arrow; Schelling's segregation model (macro-outcomes nobody chose); Hirschman's exit and voice; Marx (whose order is it?).

Selves. The agents aren't prior to the order — they're produced by it. Mead (I/me, generalized other = the group internalized — the hinge between norms and selves), Goffman (presentation of self), socialization, identity, position-shaped interests. Groups make selves make groups.

Coda. Every mechanism in the unit is a tool for the course's larger question: we are about to add a new kind of agent to the social order, and the whole toolkit applies. The four talks were this unit in narrative form.
