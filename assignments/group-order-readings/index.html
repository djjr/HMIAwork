Readings on Groups, Norms, and Decentralized Social Control

A wide-net list for the fourth "solution" in the social order segment — order produced neither by shared inner meaning, nor by price, nor by command, but by people watching, gossiping about, and sanctioning each other. Organized in clusters so you can pull a spine of three or four and let students discover the rest, or assign a cluster per week. Several entries near the end loop back to the course's AI thread, since decentralized human governance turns out to be a live design problem for multi-agent AI systems too.

Touchstones

The four books that between them make the whole case: order can arise, and often does arise, without a sovereign.

Robert Ellickson, Order Without Law: How Neighbors Settle Disputes (1991). Cattle ranchers in Shasta County, California resolve trespass disputes through neighborly norms, almost never through the law of the books — even lawyers among them don't know the actual statute. The founding fieldwork for "law and norms" as a distinct field, and a model for how one well-chosen empirical case can dismantle a theoretical assumption (here, that formal law is what keeps neighbors civil).

Elinor Ostrom, Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action (1990). Irrigation systems, fisheries, and forests that communities have self-governed for centuries, distilled into eight design principles (clear boundaries, congruence between rules and local conditions, graduated sanctions, low-cost conflict resolution, and so on). The Nobel-winning rebuttal to "tragedy of the commons" as an iron law — the tragedy is what happens absent these design principles, not absence of a state.

Robert Axelrod, The Evolution of Cooperation (1984). The tournament that made tit-for-tat famous: cooperation can be the evolutionarily stable strategy in repeated games among self-interested agents, no enforcer required. Useful to pair with a live game-theory demo in class — the argument is more convincing run than read.

James S. Coleman, Foundations of Social Theory (1990), esp. chs. 8, 12. Introduces "social capital" and "closure" — a norm is enforceable in a network only if information about violations can circulate back to everyone who matters. Coleman's Wholesale Diamond Market anecdote (traders handing over pouches of stones with a handshake) makes a nice bridge to Bernstein below.

Why humans are built for this: evolutionary and psychological foundations

Joseph Henrich, The Secret of Our Success (2015). Cultural learning and norm-following, not raw intelligence, is the trait that let humans out-compete every other species — cultural group selection favors groups whose norms happen to make them more cohesive and productive. Very readable, full of vivid failure cases (Europeans starving next to food they didn't recognize as edible).

Robert Boyd and Peter Richerson, "Group Beneficial Norms Can Spread Rapidly in a Structured Population," Journal of Theoretical Biology (2002). The formal model behind Henrich's story — shows how conformist transmission plus between-group competition lets costly, group-beneficial norms invade a population even though they're individually costly. Denser than Henrich's book but the right next step for a quantitatively curious student.

Ernst Fehr and Simon Gächter, "Altruistic Punishment in Humans," Nature (2002). The classic public-goods-game experiment: people will pay their own money to punish free-riders even when they'll never interact with them again, and cooperation collapses without that option. Arguably the single most-cited empirical grounding for "decentralized enforcement is a real human motive, not just a story."

Martin Nowak, "Five Rules for the Evolution of Cooperation," Science (2006). A tight taxonomy — kin selection, direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity, network reciprocity, group selection — that gives students vocabulary to sort every other reading in this list into a mechanism.

Jonathan Haidt, The Righteous Mind (2012), esp. ch. 11 ("Religion Is a Team Sport"). The "hive switch" — humans have a partial capacity to lose themselves in a group and prioritize its norms over individual calculation. Less rigorous than the above but the most teachable account of why groups feel like more than a sum of individually-incentivized enforcers.

The machinery of enforcement: reputation, gossip, and signaling

Cristina Bicchieri, Norms in the Wild: How to Diagnose, Measure, and Change Social Norms (2017). A working toolkit distinguishing descriptive norms ("what others do") from injunctive norms ("what others approve of") and showing how conflating them causes failed interventions (e.g., anti-littering campaigns that advertise how much littering happens). The most directly usable reading on this list if students will ever try to design a norm-based intervention themselves.

Michael Spence, "Job Market Signaling," Quarterly Journal of Economics (1973). Not about norms directly, but the foundational signaling model — costly, hard-to-fake signals sort honest from dishonest actors even with no central verifier. Every subsequent reading about reputation and honor is implicitly built on this.

Richard McElreath, Robert Boyd, and Peter Richerson, "Shared Norms and the Evolution of Ethnic Markers," Current Anthropology (2003). Models how arbitrary markers (dialect, dress, tattoos) become reliable cues for "will cooperate with me on shared norms," letting large-scale cooperation scale past the limits of personal acquaintance — the norm-enforcement analogue of Spence.

Robin Dunbar, "Gossip in Evolutionary Perspective," Review of General Psychology (2004). Gossip as the low-bandwidth reputation-transmission system that makes decentralized sanctioning possible at group sizes beyond what anyone can personally monitor — the information-infrastructure piece that Ellickson and Ostrom's field cases both quietly assume.

Order without a state: private governance among strangers

Lisa Bernstein, "Opting Out of the Legal System: Extralegal Contractual Relations in the Diamond Industry," Journal of Legal Studies (1992). New York's diamond merchants run a multibillion-dollar trade on handshakes and a private arbitration board, deliberately avoiding courts because reputational sanction is faster and more feared. A tight companion piece to Ellickson, from commerce rather than agriculture.

Avner Greif, "Reputation and Coalitions in Medieval Trade: Evidence on the Maghribi Traders," Journal of Economic History (1989). Eleventh-century Jewish traders enforced honest dealing across the Mediterranean through a multilateral reputation network — cheat one agent and the whole coalition stops trading with you. A historical-economics classic and a clean worked example of "indirect reciprocity" from Nowak's taxonomy above.

David Skarbek, The Social Order of the Underworld: How Prison Gangs Govern the American Penal System (2014). Prison gangs as governance institutions that emerge precisely because the state has abdicated order-keeping inside prisons — property rights, contract enforcement, and dispute resolution all provided extralegally. A useful, uncomfortable case: decentralized self-governance is not inherently benign.

Peter Leeson, The Invisible Hook: The Hidden Economics of Pirates (2009). Pirate ships ran on written constitutions, checks on the captain's power, and disability compensation schemes, all self-enforced with no external authority — a genuinely fun read that still makes a rigorous public-choice argument.

Richard Nisbett and Dov Cohen, Culture of Honor: The Psychology of Violence in the American South (1996). Herding economies (where livestock is stealable and the state can't protect it) generate honor norms and violent self-help enforcement that persist for generations after the economic conditions that produced them are gone. Good for showing that decentralized control mechanisms can outlive their original function — a norm as a kind of stuck equilibrium.

Zooming out: institutions and the state's shadow

Gretchen Helmke and Steven Levitsky, "Informal Institutions and Comparative Politics: A Research Agenda," Perspectives on Politics (2004). A typology of how informal and formal institutions relate — complementary, accommodating, competing, substitutive — that gives students a framework for classifying every case in this list against the state, rather than treating "decentralized" as one undifferentiated bucket.

James C. Scott, The Art of Not Being Governed: An Anarchist History of Upland Southeast Asia (2009). Whole societies (Zomia) structured their agriculture, kinship, and even illiteracy specifically to evade state incorporation — statelessness as an active, ongoing achievement rather than a primitive default. Pairs well with Scott's Seeing Like a State if you want a matched pair on hierarchy's failures vs. its evasion.

Networks: how structure carries the norm

Mark Granovetter, "The Strength of Weak Ties," American Journal of Sociology (1973). The network-structure argument: strong ties bind small cliques together, but the weak ties bridging cliques are what carry information (and norms) across a whole society. Essential vocabulary before any discussion of how a norm actually diffuses.

Mark Granovetter, "Threshold Models of Collective Behavior," American Journal of Sociology (1978). Individuals join a riot, a strike, or a norm cascade once enough others already have — and wildly different aggregate outcomes can follow from tiny differences in the threshold distribution, even with identical individuals. A great in-class simulation exercise, not just a reading.

Timur Kuran, Private Truths, Public Lies: The Social Consequences of Preference Falsification (1995). People publicly comply with norms they privately reject, which hides how fragile a norm actually is until a shock reveals the true distribution of preferences all at once (Kuran's model anticipates the 1989 Eastern Bloc collapses). The dark mirror of Granovetter's thresholds — norm enforcement can look robust right up until it isn't.

The digital frontier: decentralized governance online

Robert Kraut and Paul Resnick, Building Successful Online Communities: Evidence-Based Social Design (2012). Translates the social-science literature (much of it above) into concrete design recommendations for online moderation, newcomer socialization, and norm-setting — the applied-engineering counterpart to Bicchieri and Ostrom. Good if the course wants students to actually design a governance mechanism as an assignment.

Yochai Benkler, The Wealth of Networks (2006). Commons-based peer production (Wikipedia, open-source software) as a genuinely new fourth mode of production alongside firms and markets, coordinated by norms and reputation rather than either price or hierarchy — arguably the natural bridge reading between this segment and the "markets" and "hierarchy" segments elsewhere in the module.

Charles Schweik and Robert English, Internet Success: A Study of Open-Source Software Commons (2012). Takes Ostrom's design principles from irrigation systems and forests and tests them empirically against thousands of open-source projects, asking which succeed and why. The most direct "does Ostrom's framework generalize past physical commons" test on this list.

Back to the machines: decentralized governance for AI agents

Tomer Jordi Chaffer, Charles von Goins II, Dontrail Cotlage, Bayo Okusanya, and Justin Goldston, "Decentralized Governance of AI Agents," arXiv:2412.17114 (2024). Proposes ETHOS, a Web3-based governance framework for autonomous AI agents — a global registry, dynamic risk classification, soulbound tokens, and decentralized dispute resolution — arguing agents need governance mechanisms in the spirit of the human decentralized-order literature above (reputation, sanctions, credible commitments) rather than only top-down alignment of each individual agent. A clean way to close the segment by turning the whole reading list back into a design brief for the course's other half.

"Governance-as-a-Service: A Multi-Agent Framework for AI System Compliance and Policy Enforcement," arXiv:2508.18765 (2025). A concrete engineering proposal for norm enforcement among AI systems — worth pairing with the Chan et al. paper as a "here's what people are actually trying to build" companion, and worth asking students to evaluate against Ostrom's design principles directly.

Counterpoints: when decentralized control isn't benign

Jo Freeman, "The Tyranny of Structurelessness" (1972). A movement-organizing classic arguing that groups that refuse formal hierarchy don't eliminate power, they just make it informal, unaccountable, and invisible to newcomers — the single best skeptical counter-reading to put against Ostrom and Ellickson's more admiring cases. Short enough to assign alongside anything else in this list without displacing it.

Alejandro Portes, "Social Capital: Its Origins and Applications in Modern Sociology," Annual Review of Sociology (1998). A review that takes social capital seriously while cataloguing its downsides — the same network closure that enforces norms inside a group (Coleman, above) also excludes outsiders, enforces conformity, and can trap members (gang loyalty, ethnic enclaves that limit mobility). The clean synthesis piece if you want one reading that names the trade-off Skarbek's prison gangs and Nisbett & Cohen's honor culture both dramatize.

If you can only assign three

Ellickson for the founding empirical case, Fehr & Gächter for the experimental grounding of why people enforce norms at personal cost, and Freeman for the immediate skeptical pushback — that trio alone stages the whole debate (decentralized order works, here's the mechanism, here's what it costs) in under 100 pages combined.

A note on sourcing

Web search was used to check publication details and to find a few items (the multi-agent AI governance papers, the Cultural Evolution Society's own reading list, which several of the norm-signaling papers above were cross-checked against) that postdate or sit outside easy recall. Full citations are given so you can verify editions and pull PDFs/DOIs before finalizing a syllabus.
