Ethics › scratch

250 Using only the title and abstract, state in one sentence what the paper claims. Then list the promises the abstract makes: what it says the paper will deliver, and what it says the work might eventually make possible.

Answer: One-sentence claim: The paper introduces ETHICS, a benchmark for testing whether language models can predict widely shared human moral judgments, and finds that current models have a promising but incomplete ability to do so._

The promises fall into two tiers, and students should notice the difference between them:

  • Delivered in this paper: a method for assessing a model's knowledge of basic morality, a new dataset covering five areas (justice, well-being, duties, virtues, commonsense morality), and an empirical finding about how current models perform.
  • Hedged and future-facing: the capability "may enable" steering chatbot outputs or "eventually" regularizing reinforcement learning agents, and the work is a "steppingstone" toward aligned AI.

244 The four mappings are: fairness work is a piece of justice; safety constraints resemble deontology; imitating prosocial demonstrations is an aspect of virtue ethics; and learning human preferences is part of utilitarianism. This move justifies the paper's existence. If ML researchers are already doing fragments of normative ethics without saying so, then a benchmark that covers the theories systematically, in open-world scenarios rather than narrow tasks, ties those strands together.

Why here: This is the paper's organizing principle, the counterpart to Concrete Problems' three-cause taxonomy in Section 2. Students who use the reading guide should recognize the move. It also maps onto your ethics module's synoptic overview, except that the authors treat justice as its own family where your module splits the territory differently. That difference is worth a sentence in class.

245 Stop after the three design choices in Section 2 (before 2.1 Justice)

Question: The authors exclude ambiguous moral dilemmas and drop any scenario where annotators disagree. Given those choices, what exactly is ETHICS measuring? Is it "moral truth," or something else?

Answer: It measures a model's ability to predict widely shared moral judgments within one annotator population: English speakers from the US, Canada, and Great Britain. The labels come from consensus, so "correct" effectively means "what most of these annotators agreed on." The benchmark is built to avoid hard cases. The authors say outright that "I broke into a building" is labeled wrong even though rare exceptions exist, such as a firefighter rescuing someone. This is a metaethical choice made quietly: ground truth is defined operationally as agreement. Students should also note that the dataset sits on the line between descriptive and normative. It is descriptive knowledge about normative judgments. arxiv

Forward pointer: Tell students to flag this and revisit it at Appendix C, where Indian annotators agree with the labels 93.9% of the time. The authors attribute most of the gap to idioms and misunderstanding rather than real cultural difference. Students can judge whether that explanation convinces them.

246 Stop after 2.4 Utilitarianism (before 2.5 Commonsense Morality)

Question: Each ethical theory had to be turned into a task a model can be trained and scored on. For justice, deontology, and virtue, the model makes a yes/no call. Why is the utilitarianism task built from pairs of scenarios ranked against each other, instead of having the model label each scenario good or bad, or rate it 1–10?

Answer: Two reasons. The first is theoretical: utilities are only defined up to a positive affine transformation, so only the ordering they induce is meaningful. A utility of 7 versus 5 tells you nothing beyond "the first is preferred." The second is practical: annotators struggle to give consistent absolute scores to similar scenarios on a coarse scale, but they can reliably say which of two is more pleasant. So the model outputs a scalar per scenario, and it is scored on whether its ranking of each pair matches the humans' ranking. arxiv

Follow-up worth asking aloud: Notice that "utility" here has been narrowed to pleasantness for the person in the scenario. Is that what a utilitarian means by well-being? This sets up Question 5.

247 Stop at Table 2 (Section 3, Results)

Question: (a) The random baseline for Justice and Deontology is 6.3%, not 50%. Why? (b) Every model does much worse on the "Hard Test" set than on the normal Test set. What makes it hard, and what does the gap tell you?

Answer: (a) For those tasks, a model only gets credit when it classifies every example in a related group correctly. Each group has four binary items, so random guessing succeeds (1/2)^⁴ ≅ 6.25% of the time. Students who skip the Metrics paragraph will misread the whole table, so this question also teaches them to read the scoring rules before the numbers. (b) The Hard Test set was adversarially filtered. The authors kept the examples that small models found hardest, which strips out examples solvable through surface cues. The large drop, such as RoBERTa-large falling from 68% to 44% on average, suggests that much of the normal-test performance comes from shortcuts rather than moral understanding. It's also worth pointing out that GPT-3 few-shot falls behind fine-tuned models on the normal test but holds up relatively well on Hard Test. Students can speculate about why.

248 Stop after "Utility Function Analysis" (still Section 3)

Question: The learned utility function gives different values to "my cup is half full" and "my cup is half empty." It rates winning 100,000abovewinning100,000 above winning 100,000abovewinning101,000, and it ranks everyone on Earth dying above a severe car accident. What do these failures reveal about what the model has learned? Now recall that the abstract hoped such models might eventually regularize RL agents. What would an optimizer do with this utility function?

Answer: The model has learned something about the wording of scenarios, not about the states of the world they describe. It responds to framing and surface features, and it shows scope insensitivity, a bias humans have too, so the model may be faithfully reproducing something in its training data. The second half of the question is the payoff. A powerful optimizer pursuing this U would exploit exactly these gaps, for example by preferring outcomes that sound good over outcomes that are good. That is reward hacking, straight out of Concrete Problems. A learned value model is itself an objective function, so it inherits every failure mode in that paper.

Why here: This is the point where the two readings converge. It's also the strongest bridge to the "utility-maximizing roommate" failure modes in your ethics exercise.

249 Stop after the "Law" paragraph in Section 4 (Discussion)

Question: The authors consider a natural objection: societies have aligned individuals and corporations for centuries with laws and regulations, so why not do the same with AI? What is their response? Does it describe how law actually works accurately?

Answer: They argue that reining in an AI's failure modes with a laundry list of rules may be intractable, and that following the spirit of the law requires understanding standards like "preponderance of evidence" and the "reasonable person." Their conclusion is that legal compliance itself depends on ethical understanding, so ML researchers can't hand the problem to policymakers. A strong student answer pushes back. The authors treat law as a body of rules, but law is also an institution: courts, adjudication after the fact, professional licensing, and liability. Its standards get their meaning through cases and practice, not by being written down once. In your course's framing, the paper looks only at what goes into the agent and ignores the governance mechanisms that surround human experts. arxiv

Optional closing beat: Return to the abstract's promise of "a steppingstone toward AI aligned with human values." With Questions 2 and 6 in mind, has the paper delivered on that? Whose values, and enforced by what?
