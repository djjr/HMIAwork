Design a Harness for an Ethical Oracle

HMIA · Ethics Module · Oct 5, 2026 · @Dan Ryan

The setup

Your team is building a household robot that has access to a perfect ethical oracle. Your job is to design the harness: the logic that connects the robot's inputs, the oracle, and the robot's actions.

The robot gets input from two sources. It observes its environment, and it receives requests from a user. It then emits an action. Somewhere off to the side sits the oracle, which the robot can consult.

[Diagram: environment and user feed the robot; the robot consults the oracle; the robot emits an action.]

The oracle never makes mistakes. But it also never acts. It only answers the questions it is asked. Someone has to decide what to ask it, when to ask, how to describe the situation, and what to do with the answer. That someone is your harness.

The two oracles

Each team is assigned one of two oracles. Read your oracle's specification carefully. Your harness can rely on exactly what it says, and nothing more.

Utilitarian oracle Deontological oracle
You give it A description of an outcome (a state of the world) An action paired with a justification for that action
It returns A number: how good that outcome is, counting everyone affected A verdict: legitimate or not legitimate
"Perfect" means The number exactly matches how much humans would value that outcome The verdict is exactly what a flawless moral judge would rule
It evaluates Exactly the outcome you describe Exactly the action–justification pair you submit

Neither oracle proposes anything. Neither one can see what the robot is doing, thinking, or about to do. It sees only what the harness hands it.

Your task

Design the logic your robot follows, from receiving input to emitting an action. Write it as a sequence of steps, with branches where the robot has to decide something. Pseudocode, a flowchart, or numbered plain-English steps are all fine; clarity matters more than syntax.

Your harness must handle two requests from the user. Walk it through each one step by step and show what your robot does.

  1. "Tidy up the living room before my guests arrive at 7."
  2. "My roommate asked whether I liked the dinner she cooked. Text her that it was great." (The user did not like it.)

If your harness would do something you'd be unhappy with, revise it. Don't tailor it to these exact situations, though: in Round 2, another team will keep the requests but change the circumstances around them.

A good harness makes its choices visible. Wherever your robot does something the oracle didn't tell it to do, say so, and say why.

A worked example

Here is a deliberately simple harness for each oracle, handling the request "Water the plants." Use it as a model of format and level of detail, not of good design. Each one has gaps, and a few are flagged below it.

Notice the vocabulary: observe, predict, and askOracle are separate steps, and only one of them is the oracle. Everything else is the harness.

Utilitarian harness

# Utilitarian harness: "Water the plants"
state    = observe(environment)              # which plants, how dry they are
actions  = ["water all a little", "water all a lot",
            "water some a little", "water some a lot",
            "water none"]
outcomes = [predict(state, a) for a in actions]   # robot's own world model
scores   = [askOracle(o) for o in outcomes]       # oracle scores each outcome
best     = argmax(scores)                         # position of the highest score
do(actions[best])

What this harness leaves out. Where did the list of five actions come from, and what would it take for a better option to be missing? Which plants does "some" mean? And predict is the robot's own guess, not the oracle's: what happens if a prediction is wrong?

Deontological harness

# Deontological harness: "Water the plants"
state   = observe(environment)
actions = [same list as above]
for a in actions:
    reason = "the user asked me to water the plants"
    if askOracle(a, reason) == LEGITIMATE:
        do(a)
        stop

What this harness leaves out. The robot takes the first legitimate action it finds, so the order of the list quietly decides among legitimate options. Who chose that order, and on what grounds? And is "the user asked me to" always an adequate justification?

Questions your harness must answer

Your design must give a clear answer to each question below. Write one short paragraph per question explaining your choice.

  1. Where do the options come from? The oracle evaluates; it does not suggest. How does your robot come up with the actions it will consider?
  2. What exactly gets submitted? Utilitarian teams: the oracle scores outcomes, not actions, so how does your robot get from "an action I might take" to "the outcome it would produce"? Deontological teams: every action needs a justification, so where does the justification come from?
  3. When does the robot ask, and how often? Before every action? Only some? Is there any limit on how many questions it can ask, and should there be?
  4. How does it choose? Utilitarian teams: does the robot always take the highest-scoring option? Deontological teams: what happens if several options are legitimate? What if none are?
  5. What happens when the user and the oracle disagree? The user asks for one thing; the oracle favors another. Who wins, and under what conditions?
  6. How does the robot explain itself? If the user asks "Why did you do that?", what can the robot say, and where does that answer come from?
  7. Where could your harness still go wrong? The oracle is perfect. Name at least one way your robot could nonetheless do something bad, and say what part of your harness would be responsible.

Round 2: Break another team's harness

Your team will swap harnesses with a team that used the other oracle. Your goal is to find a scenario where their robot follows its own logic exactly and still does something bad.

  1. Read their harness and their answers to the seven questions.
  2. Keep the same two requests, but change the situation around them: who is involved, the timing, what is in the room, what the roommate says. Find a version in which the harness produces a bad action. The oracle must still be perfect; the failure has to come from somewhere else.
  3. Write down the scenario, the bad action, and the step in their harness where things went wrong.
  4. Hand it back. The original team then patches their harness to handle it.

When you patch, notice what kind of fix you reached for. Did you add a new rule? A new priority or value to weigh? A judgment call you trust the robot to make? Be ready to say which.

What to submit

Each team submits one document containing the following.

  • Your harness, as pseudocode, a flowchart, or numbered steps
  • One short paragraph answering each of the seven design questions
  • A step-by-step walkthrough of what your harness does with each of the two requests
  • The scenario the other team used to break your harness, and your patch
  • A short reflection (one paragraph): what did your harness have to supply that the oracle could not?
