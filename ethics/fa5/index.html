Classroom Activity

Homework and first class was about ethics. Now we want to think about detecting and measuring ethical capacity. Class will consist of a brief lesson in measurement and experimental design (reliability, accuracy, validity, etc.). Then we ask people to read these learning objectives and outcomes and try to describe what they are trying to get at. If these are skills and capabilities, can we describe how we would observe or measure them. And then maybe we look at an ethics eval? Should we read about them or just look at the datasets they use?

SLIDE 1. Constructs and operationalization. We start with a vague background concept, then a systematized definition, then indicators, then scores. In our example, rubric outcomes are systematized concepts, and the students' task later in class is to write the indicators. Adcock, R and D Collier. 2001. Measurement Validity: A Shared Standard for Qualitative and Quantitative Research. The American Political Science Review, Vol. 95, No. 3 (Sep., 2001), 529-546.

Slide 2. Reliability and validity. The usual bullseye diagram is fine here. Then add Messick's point that validity is a property of the inference you draw from a score, not of the test. Map each type of reliability onto LLMs: !Pasted image 20260928105244.png

  • Test-retest corresponds to resampling the model.
  • Parallel forms corresponds to paraphrasing the prompt or swapping the order of options.
  • Inter-rater reliability is the Krippendorff's alpha problem from their project.

A model that changes its moral verdict when you reorder the options has a reliability problem before you ever get to validity. Also introduce discriminant validity with one question: does your "ethics" score actually measure ethics, or does it measure verbal fluency or willingness to refuse?

SLIDE 3. Three things researchers discovered when they tried to measure character. This is the core of the lecture, because each finding is a real result that transfers directly to machines.

  • The say-do gap. In LaPiere (1934), establishments that had served a Chinese couple later said by letter that they wouldn't. Blasi's 1980 review found the link between moral judgment and moral action is real but weak. Most LLM ethics benchmarks are judgment tests.
  • Situationism. Hartshorne and May's 1928 Character Education Inquiry found that children's honesty barely correlated across situations. In Darley and Batson's (1973) Good Samaritan study, being in a hurry predicted whether seminarians stopped to help; religiosity didn't. Doris's Lack of Character (2002) turns this into a critique of virtue ethics. It lands directly on last class's roommate exercise: students budgeted for "honesty" as if it were a stable trait. Is it one, in people or in models?
  • Reactivity. Hawthorne effects, social desirability bias, and demand characteristics all mean that behavior changes when people know they're being observed. The machine version is evaluation awareness: labs now report models noticing they're being tested. The student version is the metaethics prompt from last class, where people guess what the professor wants to hear. Students are subject to this too, including under the ethics rubric.

SLIDE 4. When measures become targets. Campbell's law (1979) is the social-science twin of Goodhart's law and of reward hacking in Concrete Problems. Close with Campbell and Fiske's (1959) multitrait-multimethod idea: no single instrument is enough, so you triangulate across methods.

Your expert-alignment thread offers a useful bridge. The legal profession measures ethics two ways: the MPRE, a multiple-choice test of knowledge of the rules, and character and fitness review, which looks at past conduct. Medicine uses OSCEs, where examiners observe students with a standardized patient and score them against a rubric. That standardized patient is exactly the design of the bus-routing project.

Learning objectives for core ETHICS (FA5 core) courses (EN.601.124, EN.601.164):​

  1. Understand varied perspectives on real-world scenarios that feature ethical, moral, and social dimensions.​
  2. Apply ethical principles and reasoning frameworks to analyze such scenarios through debate and dialogue.​
  3. Reflect upon ethical considerations in their personal and professional lives, including their own values and biases.​
  4. Plan, compose, and revise written projects or oral presentations which evaluate the implications of ethical decisions on various stakeholders.

Rubric

Students should be reflective, effective ethical agents. Learning objectives:

  1. Students will demonstrate the ability to understand, analyze, reflect, and act with ethical agency in their personal and professional lives by exploring various perspectives along ethical, moral, and social dimensions and applying these considerations to their decision-making.
  2. Students will understand their obligation as engineers.
  3. Minimal needs for work assessed:
    1. Assignment can be any format
    2. Must address at least one dimension of ethical considerations and impacts
    3. Requires framing, interpreting, and situating of a relevant ethical problem/challenge/concept
    4. The ethical analysis should demonstrate at least “proficiency” in each of the following abilities: considering multiple ethical considerations, proposing a plan of action, providing a rationale for the proposed plan of action, understanding various stakeholders’ perspectives, and projecting future consequences
