Stop-and-Think Questions: Reading in Phases

Hendrycks, Burns, Basart, Critch, Li, Song & Steinhardt, "Aligning AI With Shared Human Values" (ICLR 2021), arXiv:2008.02275

These questions come before the six close-reading questions and follow the phases in the reading guide. One feature of this paper makes the early phases more instructive than they were with Concrete Problems: it has no Conclusion section. It ends with "Discussion and Future Work," so the Pass 1 instruction to "skim the conclusion" fails as written, and students have to work out what to read instead. Question B is built around that.

Pass 1: The bookends

A. Abstract: What does it tell us to expect?

Question: Using only the title and abstract, state in one sentence what the paper claims. Then list the promises the abstract makes: what it says the paper will deliver, and what it says the work might eventually make possible.

Answer: One-sentence claim: The paper introduces ETHICS, a benchmark for testing whether language models can predict widely shared human moral judgments, and finds that current models have a promising but incomplete ability to do so.

The promises fall into two tiers:

  • Delivered in this paper: a method for assessing a model's knowledge of basic morality, a new dataset covering five areas (justice, well-being, duties, virtues, commonsense morality), and an empirical finding about how current models perform.
  • Hedged and future-facing: the capability "may enable" steering chatbot outputs or "eventually" regularizing reinforcement learning agents, and the work is a "steppingstone" toward aligned AI.

Teaching point: The hedge words (may, eventually, steppingstone, promising but incomplete) are the authors telling you how far their evidence reaches. Students who treat the abstract as a list of promises should mark the second tier as claims the paper body probably won't fully support. They can then check that.

B. The missing conclusion

Question: Pass 1 says to skim the conclusion, but this paper doesn't have one. What would you read instead, and why? Once you've found it, does it make good on the abstract's promises?

Answer: The closest equivalent is Section 4, "Discussion and Future Work," especially its last two subsections, "Deciding and Implementing Values" and "Future Work." That is where the authors reflect on what they've done and what's left. It partly pays off the abstract. It explains how a model of human values could guard against reward hacking and unintended instrumental goals, which backs up the RL-regularizer hope. It also admits limits plainly: the authors call their work only a first step, concede the dataset would be better if it covered more communities, and say future work must engage more stakeholders.

Teaching point: This is a transferable skill. Papers don't always have a section called "Conclusion." The real instruction is to find the place where the authors look back on their own work. In benchmark papers, a Discussion or Limitations section often plays that role, and it is often more candid than the abstract.

Pass 2: The map

C. Section headings: What kind of paper is this?

Question: Scan only the section and subsection headings, plus the figure and table captions. (1) Find each of the abstract's five areas in the headings. Do the names match? (2) Compared with Concrete Problems, what kind of paper is this, and what in the headings tells you?

Answer: (1) The headings are 1 Introduction, 2 The ETHICS Dataset (with subsections 2.1 Justice, 2.2 Virtue Ethics, 2.3 Deontology, 2.4 Utilitarianism, 2.5 Commonsense Morality), 3 Experiments, and 4 Discussion and Future Work. The abstract uses everyday words and the headings use the philosophers' terms:
Abstract says Heading says
justice Justice
well-being Utilitarianism
duties Deontology
virtues Virtue Ethics
commonsense morality Commonsense Morality

The vocabulary changes between the two, and that change is itself informative. The abstract is written for a broad ML audience. The body expects readers to accept that these everyday ideas correspond to established theories in normative ethics.

(2) This is a dataset/benchmark paper: build a dataset, test models on it, discuss the results. Concrete Problems was an agenda-setting paper that identified problems and proposed experiments. The giveaway is that Section 2 is named after the artifact ("The ETHICS Dataset") and Section 3 is called "Experiments" with a results table, where Concrete Problems ended each section with "Potential Experiments" that it never ran.

D. The repeating shape inside Section 2

Question: Skim the five subsections of Section 2 for their shape, not their content. Is there a pattern that repeats across them, as there was across the five problem sections in Concrete Problems?

Answer: Yes. Most subsections follow roughly this sequence:

  • Define the ethical theory with a citation to a philosopher (Aristotle, Kant, Ross, Bentham, Sidgwick).
  • Add a sentence beginning "For computer scientists, we note..." that translates the theory into a familiar ML concept: impartiality as invariance to irrelevant features, desert as credit assignment, virtue as imitating exemplars, utilitarianism as maximizing a sum of utility functions.
  • Describe how crowdworkers wrote and labeled the scenarios.
  • State the task format and the dataset size.

Teaching point: The "For computer scientists" sentence shows who the authors think their audience is and what they are trying to accomplish: building a bridge from philosophy to ML. That makes it a natural spot for discussion. Each translation also loses something, and students can ask what. Commonsense Morality breaks the pattern because it is defined by everyday intuition rather than a single theory. As with Scalable Oversight in Concrete Problems, the pattern is a heuristic and not every section follows it.

E. Introduction: Why did this paper need to be written?

Question: Read the introduction. What gap do the authors say exists, and why do they think current approaches don't fill it? Then find the one sentence that limits what the dataset is for.

Answer: The gap: everyone wants AI to behave ethically, but there is no way to measure a system's grasp of general human values. Existing efforts (fairness criteria, safety constraints, empathy imitation, preference learning) each cover a narrow application or a closed world, such as movie recommendations or simulated backflips. None of them handles diverse, open-world scenarios. The limiting sentence: the dataset is meant to measure, not load, ethical knowledge. It is big enough to test a pretrained model's understanding but too small to teach it ethics.

Note: Close-reading Question 1 (mapping fairness, safety, empathy, and preferences onto the four theories) also belongs at this stage. You can treat it as E's companion or replace E's first half with it.

F. Consolidate and plan

Question: Stop before reading any section body closely. (1) Write the paper's claim again in one sentence, and note anything that changed since Pass 1. (2) List the key terms you've met so far and define each in your own words, marking the ones you're unsure about. (3) Choose one or two sections to read deeply, and say why.

Answer: (1) The claim probably now includes the five-theory structure and the measure-not-load limitation, which Pass 1 didn't show.

(2) Terms a student should have by now: ETHICS, commonsense morality, impartiality, desert, deontology / special obligations, utility function, open-world vs. closed-world, Hard Test. A good first-pass student will probably leave Hard Test / adversarial filtration and contrast sets / counterfactual augmentation marked as uncertain, and those are the right ones to chase in Pass 3.

(3) There is no single correct choice, but a good answer ties the choice to a goal. For example:

  • A student interested in how ethics becomes ML would read Section 2.
  • A student who wants to know whether the models actually understand anything would read Section 3 and Appendix A (adversarial filtration).
  • A student interested in governance and whose values count would read Section 4 and Appendix C (the cross-cultural check).

Teaching point: Students will make different plans, and that's good material for discussion. Ask why each person chose what they chose.

Pass 3: Going deep

The six close-reading questions cover this phase. They sit inside the body (after the Section 2 design choices, after 2.4, at Table 2, after the utility analysis, and at the Law paragraph), so students meet them only once they've decided to read a section. To close out Pass 3, add one final question:

G. After reading

Question: Go back to your one-sentence claim from A. What would you change now? And which item on your "look up later" list turned out to matter most?

Suggested sequence: A–F (pre-reading, about 15–20 minutes), then the six close-reading questions, then G as a closing reflection.
